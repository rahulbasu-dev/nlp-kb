Skip-gram with Negative Sampling

SGNS is the heart of Word2Vec [1][2] - a revolutionary approach that learns word meanings by predicting context words from center words, using a clever negative sampling trick to make training tractable.

Neural Word Embeddings Made Efficient

💡 Intuition

Understanding Skip-gram and Negative Sampling through intuitive analogies

What is Skip-gram?

Skip-gram is a neural network architecture introduced by Mikolov et al. [1][2] that learns word representations by training on a deceptively simple task: given a word, predict the words that appear nearby.

The key insight is that words appearing in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings - an idea known as the distributional hypothesis. By learning to predict context, the model is forced to encode semantic information into the word vectors.

The Party Guest Analogy

Imagine you're at a party and you overhear snippets of conversations:

  • "I adopted a dog last week and..."
  • "My dog loves to play fetch..."
  • "The cat sleeps all day but..."
  • "I adopted a cat from the shelter..."

Even without knowing what "dog" or "cat" means, you'd notice they appear in similar contexts ("adopted", "loves", "plays"). This tells you they're related concepts. Skip-gram learns exactly this way!

Skip-gram: Predicting Context

"Given a word, what words appear nearby?"

Unlike CBOW (which predicts the center from context), Skip-gram does the reverse: it predicts each context word from the center word.

Example

Sentence: "The quick brown fox jumps"

Center word: "brown" (window size = 2)

Task: Predict "The", "quick", "fox", "jumps"

This generates 4 training examples from one center word!

Why "Skip-gram"? The model can "skip" over words - it doesn't require context words to be immediately adjacent, just within the window.

The Softmax Problem

"Why can't we just use standard neural networks?"

The naive approach uses softmax over the entire vocabulary to predict context words:

P(context | center) = softmax(scores)

Computational Disaster

With a vocabulary of 1 million words:

  • Each training step computes 1M dot products
  • Normalizes over 1M probabilities
  • Updates gradients for 1M words

This is impossibly slow! Training would take years.

The Negative Sampling Breakthrough

Negative Sampling, introduced in the foundational Word2Vec papers [1], transforms the problem from multiclass classification (predict which of V words) into binary classification (is this pair real or fake?). This technique was inspired by Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE) [5], which provides a general framework for training unnormalized models.

The True/False Quiz Analogy

Instead of asking: "Which of these 1 million words is the answer?" (multiple choice)

We ask: "Is this word pair valid?" (true/false)

  • Positive pair: ("brown", "fox") - Yes, these appear together!
  • Negative pair: ("brown", "refrigerator") - No, randomly sampled noise!

We train the model to say "yes" to real pairs and "no" to random pairs. This only requires updating a few words per step, not millions!

Positive Samples

Real (center, context) pairs from the training corpus. The model learns to assign high probability to these.

king -> queen

Negative Samples

Random "noise" words paired with the center word. Sampled proportional to word frequency^0.75.

king -> banana

Two Embedding Matrices

SGNS maintains separate embeddings for center words (W) and context words (C). Final embeddings typically use W or W+C.

Subsampling Frequent Words

Common words like "the", "is", "and" appear millions of times but provide little semantic information. SGNS uses subsampling [1] to randomly discard frequent words during training.

Subsampling Probability

P(keep) = sqrt(t / f(w)) + t / f(w)

Where f(w) is the word's frequency and t is a threshold (typically 10^-5).

  • Rare words: P(keep) close to 1 (almost always kept)
  • Common words: P(keep) much less than 1 (often discarded)

Why This Works

1. Reduces training time by skipping redundant examples

2. Increases effective window size for remaining words

3. Improves embedding quality by reducing noise from stopwords

Center word: "loved" generates pairs: (loved, the), (loved, king), (loved, the), (loved, queen)

Step 2: Generate Positive and Negative Pairs

For each positive pair, sample k negative words (typically k=5 to 15):

Center Positive Context Label Negative Samples (k=3)
loved king 1 computer, banana, zebra (label: 0)

loved the 1 guitar, mountain, coffee (label: 0)

Example: One Training Step

Let's trace through one complete update for the pair ("loved", "queen") with negative samples ["computer", "banana"].

1

Look Up Embeddings

Get the center word vector for "loved" from matrix W:

w_loved = [0.2, -0.5, 0.1, 0.8] (dim=4 for illustration)

Get context vectors from matrix C:

c_queen = [0.3, 0.4, -0.2, 0.1]

c_computer = [-0.1, 0.2, 0.5, -0.3]

c_banana = [0.4, -0.1, 0.3, 0.2]

2

Compute Dot Products

Score = w_center . c_context (dot product measures similarity)

score_queen = 0.2*0.3 + (-0.5)*0.4 + 0.1*(-0.2) + 0.8*0.1 = -0.08

score_computer = 0.2*(-0.1) + (-0.5)*0.2 + 0.1*0.5 + 0.8*(-0.3) = -0.37

score_banana = 0.2*0.4 + (-0.5)*(-0.1) + 0.1*0.3 + 0.8*0.2 = 0.32

3

Apply Sigmoid

Convert scores to probabilities: sigmoid(x) = 1 / (1 + exp(-x))

P(real | loved, queen) = sigmoid(-0.08) = 0.48

P(real | loved, computer) = sigmoid(-0.37) = 0.41

P(real | loved, banana) = sigmoid(0.32) = 0.58

4

Compute Loss and Gradients

We want: P(real|queen) = 1, P(real|computer) = 0, P(real|banana) = 0

Error for positive: 1 - 0.48 = 0.52 (push queen closer)

Error for negatives: 0 - 0.41 = -0.41, 0 - 0.58 = -0.58 (push them away)

Gradient updates push "loved" and "queen" closer while pushing "loved" away from "computer" and "banana".

The Geometry of Learning

Each update adjusts vectors in the embedding space:

  • Positive pairs: Vectors move closer together (dot product increases)
  • Negative pairs: Vectors move apart (dot product decreases)

After millions of updates, words with similar contexts cluster together!

Let's train on the positive pair ("cat", "sat") with one negative sample "mat".

1

Look Up Vectors

Center word "cat": w_cat = [0.5, -0.3]

Positive context "sat": c_sat = [-0.4, 0.1]

Negative sample "mat": c_mat = [0.2, -0.5]

2

Compute Dot Products (Similarity Scores)

Positive pair (cat, sat):

score_pos = w_cat . c_sat
          = (0.5 * -0.4) + (-0.3 * 0.1)
          = -0.20 + (-0.03)
          = -0.23

Negative pair (cat, mat):

score_neg = w_cat . c_mat
          = (0.5 * 0.2) + (-0.3 * -0.5)
          = 0.10 + 0.15
          = 0.25

3

Apply Sigmoid Function

The sigmoid function converts scores to probabilities: sigmoid(x) = 1 / (1 + e^(-x))

Positive pair prediction:

P(real | cat, sat) = sigmoid(-0.23)
                = 1 / (1 + e^0.23)
                = 1 / (1 + 1.259)
                = 0.443

Negative pair prediction:

P(real | cat, mat) = sigmoid(0.25)
                = 1 / (1 + e^-0.25)
                = 1 / (1 + 0.779)
                = 0.562

Problem!

The model predicts 44.3% chance (cat, sat) is real, but 56.2% chance (cat, mat) is real.

This is backwards! We want high probability for real pairs and low for fake pairs.

4

Compute Errors and Gradients

Error = prediction - target (we want to minimize this)

Positive pair (target = 1):

error_pos = 0.443 - 1 = -0.557

Negative pair (target = 0):

error_neg = 0.562 - 0 = 0.562

5

Gradient Update with Learning Rate = 0.1

The gradient update rule [3] pushes vectors in the right direction:

Update center word "cat" (from positive pair):

grad_w_pos = error_pos * c_sat = -0.557 * [-0.4, 0.1] = [0.223, -0.056]
w_cat_new = w_cat - lr * grad_w_pos
          = [0.5, -0.3] - 0.1 * [0.223, -0.056]
          = [0.5 - 0.022, -0.3 + 0.006]
          = [0.478, -0.294]

Update center word "cat" (from negative pair):

grad_w_neg = error_neg * c_mat = 0.562 * [0.2, -0.5] = [0.112, -0.281]
w_cat_new = [0.478, -0.294] - 0.1 * [0.112, -0.281]
          = [0.478 - 0.011, -0.294 + 0.028]
          = [0.467, -0.266]

Update context word "sat" (positive):

grad_c_sat = error_pos * w_cat = -0.557 * [0.5, -0.3] = [-0.279, 0.167]
c_sat_new = [-0.4, 0.1] - 0.1 * [-0.279, 0.167]
          = [-0.4 + 0.028, 0.1 - 0.017]
          = [-0.372, 0.083]

Update context word "mat" (negative):

grad_c_mat = error_neg * w_cat = 0.562 * [0.5, -0.3] = [0.281, -0.169]
c_mat_new = [0.2, -0.5] - 0.1 * [0.281, -0.169]
          = [0.2 - 0.028, -0.5 + 0.017]
          = [0.172, -0.483]

🔢 Mathematical Foundations

The formal definitions and gradient derivations for SGNS

The Skip-gram Objective (Full Softmax)

The original Skip-gram objective [2] maximizes the probability of context words given center words:

Skip-gram Objective
\[ J = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{-c \leq j \leq c, j \neq 0} \log P(w_{t+j} | w_t) \]

Where:

  • \( T \) = total words in corpus
  • \( c \) = context window size
  • \( w_t \) = center word at position t
  • \( w_{t+j} \) = context word at offset j

The probability is computed using softmax over the entire vocabulary:

Softmax Probability
\[ P(o | c) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{u}_o^T \mathbf{v}_c)}{\sum_{w=1}^{V} \exp(\mathbf{u}_w^T \mathbf{v}_c)} \]
  • \( \mathbf{v}_c \) = center word embedding (from matrix W)
  • \( \mathbf{u}_o \) = context word embedding (from matrix C)
  • \( V \) = vocabulary size (the expensive denominator!)

Negative Sampling Objective

Negative sampling [1] replaces the expensive softmax with a binary classification objective:

SGNS Objective (per word pair)
\[ J = \log \sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^T \mathbf{v}_c) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbb{E}_{w_i \sim P_n(w)} \left[ \log \sigma(-\mathbf{u}_{w_i}^T \mathbf{v}_c) \right] \]
  • \( \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \) is the sigmoid function
  • \( k \) = number of negative samples (typically 5-20 for small datasets, 2-5 for large ones [1])
  • \( P_n(w) \propto f(w)^{0.75} \) = noise distribution (smoothed unigram) [1]

Intuition Behind the Objective

  • First term: Maximize \( \sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^T \mathbf{v}_c) \) - push positive pairs to have high dot product
  • Second term: Maximize \( \sigma(-\mathbf{u}_{w_i}^T \mathbf{v}_c) \) - push negative pairs to have low dot product

The sigmoid converts dot products to probabilities, and we maximize log probability. For a detailed derivation of this objective, see Goldberg and Levy [3].

Gradient Updates

To train the model, we compute gradients of the loss with respect to embeddings [3]:

Gradient for Center Word Embedding
\[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{v}_c} = (\sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^T \mathbf{v}_c) - 1) \mathbf{u}_o + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma(\mathbf{u}_{w_i}^T \mathbf{v}_c) \mathbf{u}_{w_i} \]
Gradient for Positive Context Embedding
\[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{u}_o} = (\sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^T \mathbf{v}_c) - 1) \mathbf{v}_c \]
Gradient for Negative Context Embedding
\[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{u}_{w_i}} = \sigma(\mathbf{u}_{w_i}^T \mathbf{v}_c) \mathbf{v}_c \]

Gradient Update Rule

With learning rate \( \alpha \), we update:

\[ \mathbf{v}_c \leftarrow \mathbf{v}_c - \alpha \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{v}_c} \]

Note: We subtract because we're maximizing J (equivalently, minimizing -J).

The Noise Distribution

Negative samples are drawn from a modified unigram distribution [1]:

Noise Distribution
\[ P_n(w) = \frac{f(w)^{0.75}}{\sum_{w'} f(w')^{0.75}} \]

The exponent 0.75 was empirically determined to work well [1]:

  • Pure unigram (1.0): Too many common words as negatives
  • Uniform (0.0): Rare words sampled too often
  • 0.75: Sweet spot - balances frequency and coverage
Word Frequency f(w) f(w)^1.0 (Unigram) f(w)^0.75 (Smoothed) Effect
the 0.07 0.07 0.13 Relatively reduced
cat 0.001 0.001 0.006 Relatively boosted
quasar 0.00001 0.00001 0.0002 Much more likely

🔄 SGNS Training Process

Visual representation of the complete training pipeline

Process Steps Explained

1

Preprocessing

Tokenization: Split text into words. Subsampling: Randomly discard frequent words (like "the") to speed up training and improve quality.

2

Initialize Embeddings

Create two matrices: W (center word embeddings) and C (context word embeddings). Initialize with small random values ~ Uniform(-0.5/d, 0.5/d).

3

Select Center Word

Iterate through each word in the corpus as the center word. The context window defines which surrounding words become positive examples.

4

Generate Training Pairs

Positive: Each (center, context) pair from the window. Negative: Sample k random words from the noise distribution for each positive pair.

5

Compute Scores and Loss

Dot product between center and context vectors, passed through sigmoid. Loss encourages high scores for positive pairs, low for negatives.

6

Update via Gradient Descent

Compute gradients and update only the embeddings involved in this sample (sparse update). This is what makes SGNS efficient!

🐍 Python Implementation

Complete, runnable SGNS implementation from scratch

Complete SGNS Implementation

A clean, educational implementation with detailed comments. Only requires NumPy!

"""
Skip-gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) - From Scratch
======================================================
Educational implementation of Word2Vec's SGNS algorithm.
"""

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
import re


class SGNS:
    """Skip-gram with Negative Sampling word embedding model."""

    def __init__(self, embedding_dim=100, window_size=5,
                 neg_samples=5, learning_rate=0.025,
                 subsample_threshold=1e-5, min_count=5):
        """
        Args:
            embedding_dim: Dimension of word vectors
            window_size: Context window size (each side)
            neg_samples: Number of negative samples per positive
            learning_rate: Initial learning rate
            subsample_threshold: Threshold for subsampling frequent words
            min_count: Minimum word frequency to include in vocabulary
        """
        self.embedding_dim = embedding_dim
        self.window_size = window_size
        self.neg_samples = neg_samples
        self.learning_rate = learning_rate
        self.subsample_threshold = subsample_threshold
        self.min_count = min_count

        # To be initialized during training
        self.word2idx = {}
        self.idx2word = {}
        self.vocab_size = 0
        self.W = None  # Center word embeddings
        self.C = None  # Context word embeddings
        self.word_freqs = None
        self.noise_dist = None

    def tokenize(self, text):
        """Simple tokenization: lowercase and extract words."""
        return re.findall(r'[a-z]+', text.lower())

    def build_vocab(self, corpus):
        """Build vocabulary from corpus."""
        word_counts = Counter()
        for sentence in corpus:
            tokens = self.tokenize(sentence)
            word_counts.update(tokens)

        # Filter by minimum count
        vocab = [w for w, c in word_counts.items() if c >= self.min_count]

        self.word2idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
        self.idx2word = {i: w for w, i in self.word2idx.items()}
        self.vocab_size = len(vocab)

        # Compute word frequencies for noise distribution
        total = sum(word_counts[w] for w in vocab)
        self.word_freqs = np.array([word_counts[self.idx2word[i]] / total
                                     for i in range(self.vocab_size)])

        # Noise distribution: f(w)^0.75 normalized
        noise = self.word_freqs ** 0.75
        self.noise_dist = noise / noise.sum()

        print(f"Vocabulary size: {self.vocab_size}")

    def subsample_prob(self, word_idx):
        """Probability of keeping a word (subsampling frequent words)."""
        freq = self.word_freqs[word_idx]
        if freq == 0:
            return 0
        ratio = self.subsample_threshold / freq
        return min(1.0, np.sqrt(ratio) + ratio)

    def sample_negatives(self, exclude_idx):
        """Sample negative word indices from noise distribution."""
        negatives = []
        while len(negatives) < self.neg_samples:
            neg = np.random.choice(self.vocab_size, p=self.noise_dist)
            if neg != exclude_idx:
                negatives.append(neg)
        return negatives

    def sigmoid(self, x):
        """Numerically stable sigmoid."""
        return np.where(x >= 0,
                        1 / (1 + np.exp(-x)),
                        np.exp(x) / (1 + np.exp(x)))

    def train_pair(self, center_idx, context_idx, label):
        """Train on a single (center, context) pair."""
        # Get embeddings
        v_c = self.W[center_idx]   # Center word vector
        u_o = self.C[context_idx]  # Context word vector

        # Forward pass
        score = np.dot(v_c, u_o)
        pred = self.sigmoid(score)

        # Compute gradient
        error = pred - label  # For positive: want pred=1, for negative: want pred=0

        # Update embeddings (gradient descent)
        grad_c = error * u_o
        grad_o = error * v_c

        self.W[center_idx] -= self.learning_rate * grad_c
        self.C[context_idx] -= self.learning_rate * grad_o

        # Return loss for monitoring
        if label == 1:
            return -np.log(pred + 1e-10)
        else:
            return -np.log(1 - pred + 1e-10)

    def train(self, corpus, epochs=5):
        """Train SGNS on corpus."""
        # Build vocabulary
        self.build_vocab(corpus)

        # Initialize embeddings
        scale = 0.5 / self.embedding_dim
        self.W = np.random.uniform(-scale, scale,
                                    (self.vocab_size, self.embedding_dim))
        self.C = np.random.uniform(-scale, scale,
                                    (self.vocab_size, self.embedding_dim))

        # Training loop
        for epoch in range(epochs):
            total_loss = 0
            num_pairs = 0

            for sentence in corpus:
                tokens = self.tokenize(sentence)
                indices = [self.word2idx[w] for w in tokens
                          if w in self.word2idx]

                # Subsample frequent words
                indices = [idx for idx in indices
                          if np.random.random() < self.subsample_prob(idx)]

                # Generate training pairs
                for i, center_idx in enumerate(indices):
                    # Dynamic window (like original Word2Vec)
                    window = np.random.randint(1, self.window_size + 1)

                    # Context words
                    start = max(0, i - window)
                    end = min(len(indices), i + window + 1)

                    for j in range(start, end):
                        if i == j:
                            continue
                        context_idx = indices[j]

                        # Positive sample
                        loss = self.train_pair(center_idx, context_idx, 1)
                        total_loss += loss

                        # Negative samples
                        for neg_idx in self.sample_negatives(context_idx):
                            loss = self.train_pair(center_idx, neg_idx, 0)
                            total_loss += loss

                        num_pairs += 1

            avg_loss = total_loss / max(num_pairs, 1)
            print(f"Epoch {epoch+1}/{epochs}, Loss: {avg_loss:.4f}")

        return self.W

    def get_embedding(self, word):
        """Get embedding for a word."""
        if word not in self.word2idx:
            return None
        return self.W[self.word2idx[word]]

    def most_similar(self, word, top_k=10):
        """Find most similar words by cosine similarity."""
        if word not in self.word2idx:
            return []

        word_vec = self.W[self.word2idx[word]]

        # Normalize all vectors
        norms = np.linalg.norm(self.W, axis=1, keepdims=True)
        normalized = self.W / (norms + 1e-10)
        word_vec_norm = word_vec / (np.linalg.norm(word_vec) + 1e-10)

        # Cosine similarity
        similarities = np.dot(normalized, word_vec_norm)

        # Get top-k (excluding the word itself)
        top_indices = np.argsort(similarities)[::-1][1:top_k+1]

        return [(self.idx2word[i], similarities[i]) for i in top_indices]

Key Implementation Details

Two Embedding Matrices

W holds center word vectors, C holds context word vectors. After training, typically use W as final embeddings, or W + C. Levy and Goldberg [4] showed that SGNS implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI matrix.

Sparse Updates

Only update embeddings for words in the current sample (1 positive + k negatives). This is what makes SGNS scale to huge vocabularies.

Dynamic Window

Original Word2Vec samples window size uniformly from [1, max_window]. This gives closer words higher weight on average.

Numerical Stability

Use stable sigmoid implementation to avoid overflow. Add small epsilon in log to prevent log(0).

Primary References

The following papers are essential reading for understanding SGNS and its theoretical foundations:

# Reference Key Contribution
[1]

Mikolov, T., Sutskever, I., Chen, K., Corrado, G. S., & Dean, J. (2013).

"Distributed representations of words and phrases and their compositionality."

Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NIPS 2013), pp. 3111-3119.

 Introduces negative sampling, subsampling of frequent words, and phrase learning. The primary reference for SGNS as implemented in Word2Vec.
[2]

Mikolov, T., Chen, K., Corrado, G., & Dean, J. (2013).

"Efficient estimation of word representations in vector space."

ICLR Workshop 2013. arXiv:1301.3781.

 The original Word2Vec paper introducing Skip-gram and CBOW architectures. Demonstrates word analogies and semantic relationships.
[3]

Goldberg, Y., & Levy, O. (2014).

"word2vec Explained: deriving Mikolov et al.'s negative-sampling word-embedding method."

arXiv:1402.3722.

 Provides a clear mathematical derivation of the SGNS objective function and gradient updates. Excellent pedagogical resource.
[4]

Levy, O., & Goldberg, Y. (2014).

"Neural word embedding as implicit matrix factorization."

Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NIPS 2014), pp. 2177-2185.

 Shows that SGNS implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI (Pointwise Mutual Information) matrix. Bridges neural and count-based methods.
[5]

Gutmann, M., & Hyvarinen, A. (2010).

"Noise-contrastive estimation: A new estimation principle for unnormalized statistical models."

Proceedings of the 13th International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Statistics (AISTATS 2010), pp. 297-304.

 Introduces Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE), the theoretical foundation for negative sampling. Essential for understanding why negative sampling works.
