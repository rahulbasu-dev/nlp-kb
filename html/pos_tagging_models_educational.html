💡 Intuition

Why we need models beyond basic HMMs for POS tagging

Recap: POS Tagging as Sequence Labeling

In Part 1, we learned that POS tagging assigns a grammatical label (noun, verb, adjective, etc.) to every word in a sentence. We modeled this with Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), which use transition probabilities between tags and emission probabilities from tags to words.

But HMMs have significant limitations. This page explores the evolution of POS tagging models that overcome those limitations:

Rule-Based

Hand-crafted rules like "a word ending in -ly is an adverb." Brittle but interpretable.

Statistical (HMM)

Generative model: learns P(words, tags). Uses Viterbi for decoding. ~95% accuracy.

Discriminative (MEMM/CRF)

Models P(tags | words) directly. Rich features. ~96–97% accuracy.

Neural (BiLSTM, BERT)

Learns features automatically from data. Bidirectional context. ~97.5–98% accuracy.

Why HMMs Aren't Enough

  • Independence assumption: HMMs assume the observed word depends only on the current tag. It cannot look at neighboring words, word shape, prefixes, or suffixes.
  • Generative vs. discriminative: HMMs model the joint P(words, tags), but we only need the conditional P(tags | words). Discriminative models are more direct and often more accurate.
  • Limited context: Standard HMMs use only bigram tag context (previous tag). They cannot use the word two positions back, or the word two positions ahead.

🔍 The Bidirectional Reading Analogy

Imagine reading a sentence left-to-right and encountering the word "bank." You might guess it's a financial institution. But if you could also peek ahead and see "...of the river," you'd instantly know it's a river bank. Reading forwards AND backwards gives more context—and that's the key insight behind bidirectional models.

✏️ Try It Yourself!

Consider: "The old man the boats."

A forward-only model might tag "old" as JJ (adjective) and "man" as NN (noun). But the correct parse has "old" as NN (noun, meaning "elderly people") and "man" as VB (verb, meaning "to operate"). What information from the right context would help a bidirectional model get this right?

Solution: Seeing "the boats" at the end reveals that something must be a verb governing "boats" as its object. A bidirectional model reading backwards would recognize that "man" needs to be a verb for the sentence to be grammatical, and "old" must therefore be a noun (subject).

📊 The Forward Algorithm

Computing the likelihood of an observation sequence efficiently

The Problem: P(O|λ)

Given an HMM λ = (A, B, π) and an observation sequence O = o1, o2, ..., oT, how do we compute the total probability of seeing that sequence? This is the evaluation problem—one of the three fundamental HMM problems.

The Naive Approach: Exponential Complexity

We could enumerate every possible state sequence Q = q1, ..., qT and sum:

Naive Computation
\[ P(O|\lambda) = \sum_{\text{all } Q} P(O|Q, \lambda) \cdot P(Q|\lambda) \]

With N hidden states and T observations, there are NT possible state sequences. For N=10 states and T=20 words, that's 1020 sequences—completely intractable!

The Forward Variable

The forward variable αt(j) captures the probability of having seen the first t observations and being in state j at time t:

Forward Variable Definition
\[ \alpha_t(j) = P(o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_t, \; q_t = j \mid \lambda) \]

The Three Steps

1. Initialization (t = 1)
\[ \alpha_1(j) = \pi_j \cdot b_j(o_1) \]

The probability of starting in state j (πj) times the probability of state j emitting the first observation (bj(o1)).

2. Recursion (t = 2, ..., T)
\[ \alpha_t(j) = \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \right] \cdot b_j(o_t) \]

Sum over all states i that could have been the previous state: take αt-1(i) (probability of being in state i at t-1), multiply by aij (transition probability from i to j), then multiply by bj(ot) (emission probability).

3. Termination
\[ P(O|\lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_T(j) \]

The total sequence probability is the sum of all forward variables at the final time step.

Complexity: O(N²T) vs O(NT)

The Forward Algorithm reuses intermediate computations (dynamic programming). At each of T time steps, we compute N states, each requiring a sum over N previous states: O(N²T)—polynomial instead of exponential.

Worked Example

Let's use a weather/ice cream example with 2 states (Hot, Cold) and 3 observations (3, 1, 3 ice creams eaten):

  • States: Hot (H), Cold (C)
  • π = [0.8, 0.2] (start probabilities)
  • Transition matrix A: aHH=0.6, aHC=0.4, aCH=0.5, aCC=0.5
  • Emission B: bH(1)=0.2, bH(2)=0.4, bH(3)=0.4; bC(1)=0.5, bC(2)=0.4, bC(3)=0.1

Observation sequence: O = (3, 1, 3)

Step 1: Initialization (t=1, o1=3)

  • α1(H) = πH · bH(3) = 0.8 × 0.4 = 0.32
  • α1(C) = πC · bC(3) = 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.02

Step 2: Recursion (t=2, o2=1)

  • α2(H) = [α1(H)·aHH + α1(C)·aCH] · bH(1) = [0.32×0.6 + 0.02×0.5] × 0.2 = [0.192 + 0.01] × 0.2 = 0.0404
  • α2(C) = [α1(H)·aHC + α1(C)·aCC] · bC(1) = [0.32×0.4 + 0.02×0.5] × 0.5 = [0.128 + 0.01] × 0.5 = 0.069

Step 3: Recursion (t=3, o3=3)

  • α3(H) = [0.0404×0.6 + 0.069×0.5] × 0.4 = [0.02424 + 0.0345] × 0.4 = 0.02350
  • α3(C) = [0.0404×0.4 + 0.069×0.5] × 0.1 = [0.01616 + 0.0345] × 0.1 = 0.005066

Termination

P(O|λ) = α3(H) + α3(C) = 0.02350 + 0.005066 = 0.02856

🔍 Viterbi Decoding

Finding the single best state sequence

Forward vs. Viterbi: Sum vs. Max

The Forward Algorithm sums over all paths to get the total probability. The Viterbi Algorithm maximizes to find the single most likely state sequence. The structure is identical—only the aggregation operation changes.

AspectForward AlgorithmViterbi Algorithm
GoalP(O|λ) — total likelihoodBest state sequence Q*
OperationSum (Σ)Max
BacktrackingNot neededBackpointers required
ComplexityO(N²T)O(N²T)
Use caseModel evaluation, trainingDecoding (tagging)

Viterbi Formulas

Viterbi Variable
\[ v_t(j) = \max_{i} \left[ v_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \right] \cdot b_j(o_t) \]

Instead of summing over all previous states, we take the maximum. This gives the probability of the best path ending in state j at time t.

Backpointer
\[ bt_t(j) = \arg\max_{i} \left[ v_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \right] \]

We also record which previous state gave the maximum. After reaching the end, we trace back through these pointers to recover the best path.

Log-Space Computation

In practice, multiplying many small probabilities leads to numerical underflow (values too small for floating-point). The solution: work in log space.

Viterbi in Log Space
\[ \log v_t(j) = \max_{i} \left[ \log v_{t-1}(i) + \log a_{ij} \right] + \log b_j(o_t) \]

Multiplications become additions, which are numerically stable. The argmax is unchanged since log is monotonic.

Worked Example

Using the same weather HMM and O = (3, 1, 3):

Initialization (t=1, o=3)

  • v1(H) = 0.8 × 0.4 = 0.32
  • v1(C) = 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.02

t=2, o=1

  • v2(H) = max(0.32×0.6, 0.02×0.5) × 0.2 = max(0.192, 0.01) × 0.2 = 0.192 × 0.2 = 0.0384, bt=H
  • v2(C) = max(0.32×0.4, 0.02×0.5) × 0.5 = max(0.128, 0.01) × 0.5 = 0.128 × 0.5 = 0.064, bt=H

t=3, o=3

  • v3(H) = max(0.0384×0.6, 0.064×0.5) × 0.4 = max(0.02304, 0.032) × 0.4 = 0.032 × 0.4 = 0.0128, bt=C
  • v3(C) = max(0.0384×0.4, 0.064×0.5) × 0.1 = max(0.01536, 0.032) × 0.1 = 0.032 × 0.1 = 0.0032, bt=C

Backtrace

Best final state: H (0.0128 > 0.0032). Trace back: t=3→H, bt=C; t=2→C, bt=H; t=1→H.

Best path: H → C → H

⚠️ Greedy vs. Viterbi

Picking the most likely state at each time step independently (greedy) can give a different answer than Viterbi. Greedy ignores transition probabilities between consecutive choices. The Viterbi path is globally optimal, not just locally optimal at each step.

⚙️ Maximum Entropy Markov Model

Discriminative sequence labeling with rich features

Motivation: Limitations of HMMs

HMMs model P(words, tags) generatively. This means:

  • The emission probability bj(ot) depends only on the current tag—we cannot condition on neighboring words, word shape, prefixes, or any other features.
  • Adding features requires changing the model structure entirely.

MEMMs flip the script: they model P(tagi | tagi-1, observation) directly, and can incorporate arbitrary features.

Generative vs. Discriminative

AspectGenerative (HMM)Discriminative (MEMM)
ModelsP(words, tags) = P(tags) · P(words|tags)P(tags | words) directly
FeaturesOnly word identityArbitrary: suffixes, shape, neighbors, etc.
IndependenceWords independent given tagsNo such assumption needed
TrainingCount-based (MLE)Logistic regression / MaxEnt
Unknown wordsRequires smoothing hacksFeature-based: handles naturally

The MEMM Model

At each position i, the MEMM models:

MEMM Local Classifier
\[ P(t_i \mid t_{i-1}, o) = \frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{f}(t_i, t_{i-1}, o)\right)}{\sum_{t'} \exp\left(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{f}(t', t_{i-1}, o)\right)} \]

This is a softmax (maximum entropy) classifier. f is a feature vector, w is a learned weight vector. The normalization is local—per position.

Feature Engineering

The power of MEMMs comes from rich features. Common POS tagging features include:

  • Current word: wi = "running"
  • Previous tag: ti-1 = VBD
  • Word suffixes: -ing, -ed, -ly, -tion, -ness
  • Word prefixes: un-, re-, pre-
  • Word shape: Xx (capitalized), XXXX (all caps), x.x (abbreviation with period)
  • Capitalization: is_capitalized, is_all_caps, is_mixed_case
  • Neighboring words: wi-1, wi+1
  • Contains digit: has_digit, is_number
  • Contains hyphen: is_hyphenated

✅ Feature Example

For the word "running" with previous tag VBD:

  • f1: word="running" & tag=VBG → 1
  • f2: suffix="-ing" & tag=VBG → 1
  • f3: prev_tag=VBD & tag=VBG → 1
  • f4: is_lowercase & tag=VBG → 1

The model learns weights for thousands of such features from training data.

The Label Bias Problem

MEMMs have a critical flaw: label bias. Because normalization is local (per state), states with few outgoing transitions effectively ignore observations.

⚠️ Label Bias Explained

Imagine a state that can only transition to one other state. No matter what word is observed, all probability mass goes to that single successor. The observation is ignored. This happens because the probabilities must sum to 1 at each state, and if there's only one option, it gets all the mass. CRFs solve this with global normalization.

Useful features:

  • prefix="un-": common adjective prefix (unhappy, unusual, unlikely)
  • suffix="-able": strong indicator of adjective (readable, comfortable)
  • word_length > 10: longer words are often adjectives or nouns
  • contains no digits/hyphens: rules out number/compound
  • is_lowercase: not a proper noun
  • prev_word context: if preceded by "is" or "an", strongly suggests JJ

↔️ Bidirectionality & CRFs

Using full context and global normalization

Why Left-to-Right Isn't Enough

Consider these sentences where right context is crucial:

  • "The lead paint was dangerous." — "lead" is JJ (adjective), but only "paint" tells us that.
  • "Please record the results." vs. "This is a record." — The word after "record" disambiguates verb vs. noun.
  • "The old man the boats." — Only "the boats" at the end reveals "man" is a verb.

Bidirectional Approaches

A bidirectional model runs two passes:

  1. Forward pass: processes the sentence left-to-right
  2. Backward pass: processes the sentence right-to-left

The predictions combine information from both directions at each position.

Conditional Random Fields (CRFs)

CRFs solve both the label bias problem and the directionality limitation. They use global normalization over the entire sequence:

Linear-Chain CRF
\[ P(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{x})} \exp\left( \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{k} \lambda_k f_k(y_t, y_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}, t) \right) \]

Where Z(x) is the partition function (normalization constant) that sums over all possible tag sequences:

Partition Function
\[ Z(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{y}'} \exp\left( \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{k} \lambda_k f_k(y'_t, y'_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}, t) \right) \]

This global normalization means the model considers the entire sequence when making predictions, avoiding label bias.

🧠 Neural Network Models

From handcrafted features to learned representations

Word Embeddings as Input

Instead of discrete word identities, neural taggers use dense word embeddings (see Word2Vec guide). Each word wt is represented as a vector xt ∈ ℝd that captures semantic and syntactic properties.

Window-Based Feedforward Tagger

The simplest neural approach: concatenate embeddings of the target word and its neighbors (e.g., a 5-word window), feed into a feedforward network, and predict the tag with softmax. This is simple but limited—fixed window size means limited context.

BiLSTM Tagger

The Bidirectional LSTM processes the entire sentence in both directions:

BiLSTM Architecture
\[ \vec{h}_t = \text{LSTM}_f(x_t, \vec{h}_{t-1}) \] \[ \overleftarrow{h}_t = \text{LSTM}_b(x_t, \overleftarrow{h}_{t+1}) \] \[ \hat{y}_t = \text{softmax}(W \cdot [\vec{h}_t ; \overleftarrow{h}_t] + b) \]

The forward LSTM reads left-to-right; the backward LSTM reads right-to-left. Their hidden states are concatenated [;] at each position, giving a representation that captures full bidirectional context. A linear layer + softmax produces tag probabilities.

BiLSTM-CRF

Adding a CRF layer on top of the BiLSTM combines the best of both worlds:

  • BiLSTM: Learns rich contextual representations automatically
  • CRF layer: Enforces valid tag transition constraints (e.g., I-PER shouldn't follow B-LOC) and optimizes the whole sequence jointly

The CRF uses BiLSTM outputs as emission scores and learns transition scores between tags. Decoding uses Viterbi over these combined scores.

Transformer-Based Taggers

Self-attention allows each word to attend to all other words simultaneously—no sequential bottleneck. Pre-trained models like BERT capture deep contextual knowledge:

  1. Take a pre-trained BERT model
  2. Add a simple linear classification layer on top
  3. Fine-tune on POS tagging data

BERT's bidirectional attention across all layers gives it extremely rich representations for each token.

🐍 Python Implementations

Forward Algorithm, MEMM, and BiLSTM tagger code

Forward Algorithm

This class shows the Forward algorithm as a stand-alone component — useful when you only need P(O|λ) for model comparison or scoring, not full decoding.

from typing import Dict, List, Tuple


# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
#  ForwardAlgorithm
#  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
#  Answers the "Evaluation" problem: given model λ=(A,B,π) and
#  observation sequence O, compute P(O | λ).
#
#  Why bother?
#    • Model selection: which of two trained HMMs better explains O?
#    • Anomaly detection: a very low P(O|λ) flags an unusual sentence.
#    • Baum-Welch normaliser: the M-step divides by P(O|λ).
#
#  Key insight — re-using partial results:
#    Brute-force: sum over N^T paths (N=states, T=length).
#    Forward DP:  O(N²T) — reuse α_{t-1} for all t transitions.
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
class ForwardAlgorithm:
    """Compute P(O | λ) via the Forward (α) dynamic programming algorithm.

    Args:
        states: List of hidden state names (e.g. ['Hot', 'Cold']).
        pi:     Initial state distribution, π[s] = P(q_1 = s).
        A:      Transition matrix, A[i][j] = P(q_t=j | q_{t-1}=i).
        B:      Emission matrix,   B[s][o] = P(o_t=o | q_t=s).
    """

    def __init__(self,
                 states: List[str],
                 pi: Dict[str, float],
                 A:  Dict[str, Dict[str, float]],
                 B:  Dict[str, Dict[str, float]]):
        self.states = states
        self.pi     = pi
        self.A      = A
        self.B      = B

    def compute(self,
               observations: List[str],
               verbose: bool = False
    ) -> Tuple[float, List[Dict[str, float]]]:
        """Run the Forward algorithm on an observation sequence.

        Three phases:
          1. Initialization  — α_1(j) = π_j · B[j][o_1]
          2. Recursion       — α_t(j) = [Σ_i α_{t-1}(i) · A[i][j]] · B[j][o_t]
          3. Termination     — P(O|λ) = Σ_j α_T(j)

        Args:
            observations: Sequence of observed symbols (must be in B's vocabulary).
            verbose:      If True, print each α column as it is computed.

        Returns:
            (total_prob, alpha_table)
            total_prob  = P(O | λ)
            alpha_table = list of dicts; alpha_table[t][state] = α_t(state).
        """
        T     = len(observations)
        alpha: List[Dict[str, float]] = []

        # ── Phase 1: Initialization ──────────────────────────────────
        # Each state j "starts" with weight π_j, then immediately
        # emits the first observation.
        a0 = {j: self.pi[j] * self.B[j][observations[0]]
              for j in self.states}
        alpha.append(a0)

        if verbose:
            self._print_step(0, observations[0], a0)

        # ── Phase 2: Recursion ───────────────────────────────────────
        for t in range(1, T):
            at = {}
            for j in self.states:
                # Collect all probability mass flowing INTO state j
                # from every predecessor state i (this is the SUM).
                incoming = sum(
                    alpha[t - 1][i] * self.A[i][j]
                    for i in self.states
                )
                # Scale by how likely state j is to emit this observation.
                at[j] = incoming * self.B[j][observations[t]]
            alpha.append(at)

            if verbose:
                self._print_step(t, observations[t], at)

        # ── Phase 3: Termination ─────────────────────────────────────
        # Sum final α values across all states: we don't care which
        # state we ended in, only the total probability of O.
        total = sum(alpha[T - 1][j] for j in self.states)

        if verbose:
            print(f"\n  P(O | λ) = {' + '.join(f'α_T({j})' for j in self.states)}")
            print(f"           = {' + '.join(f'{alpha[T-1][j]:.5f}' for j in self.states)}")
            print(f"           = {total:.6f}\n")

        return total, alpha

    def _print_step(self, t: int, obs: str, at: Dict[str, float]) -> None:
        """Print a single column of the alpha trellis."""
        print(f"  t={t} (obs='{obs}'):")
        for j, val in sorted(at.items()):
            print(f"    α_{t+1}({j}) = {val:.6f}")

MEMM Tagger

The MEMM replaces the generative HMM with a discriminative log-linear model. At each position it predicts P(tag | word, context, prev_tag) directly, conditioning on a rich feature vector. This avoids the HMM's independence assumption that emissions depend only on the current tag.

from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.feature_extraction import DictVectorizer
from typing import Dict, List, Optional, Tuple
import numpy as np


# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
#  MEMMTagger  — Maximum Entropy Markov Model
#  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
#  At each position t the model predicts:
#
#    P(tag_t | word_t, word_{t-1}, word_{t+1}, tag_{t-1})
#                  ↑ discriminative — conditions on anything!
#
#  Contrast with HMM (generative), which only models:
#    P(word_t | tag_t)  ×  P(tag_t | tag_{t-1})
#
#  Key limitation: LABEL BIAS — because the model normalises
#  locally at each step, states with fewer outgoing transitions
#  tend to "absorb" probability mass regardless of evidence.
#  CRFs fix this with a single global normalisation.
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
class MEMMTagger:
    """Greedy left-to-right MEMM tagger using logistic regression.

    Features used:
      - Current word (lowercased)
      - Suffixes of length 2 and 3  (morphology signal: -ing, -ed, -ly)
      - Prefix of length 2           (un-, re- signal)
      - Orthographic shape flags     (capitalisation, digits, hyphens)
      - Previous tag                 (local sequence context)
      - Previous word / next word    (window context)
      - Unknown-word flag
    """

    def __init__(self, C: float = 1.0):
        """Args:
            C: Regularisation strength for logistic regression (inverse).
               Smaller C → stronger regularisation (less overfitting).
        """
        self.model:      Optional[LogisticRegression] = None
        self.vectorizer: Optional[DictVectorizer]     = None
        self.tag_set:    List[str] = []
        self.word_set:   set       = set()
        self.C:          float     = C

    def extract_features(self,
                          word: str,
                          prev_tag: str,
                          prev_word: str,
                          next_word: str) -> Dict[str, float]:
        """Build a feature vector for one (word, context) pair.

        Each feature is a string key → float value (0 or 1 for indicators).
        The DictVectorizer converts these to a sparse matrix row.

        Morphological features are powerful for unseen words:
          "unforgettable" is unseen, but suffix "-able" strongly implies JJ.
          "running" is unseen, but suffix "-ing" implies VBG.
        """
        w = word  # shorthand
        features: Dict[str, float] = {
            # ── Identity ────────────────────────────────────────────
            f'word={w.lower()}':          1.0,

            # ── Morphological suffixes (most informative for OOV words)
            # "-ing" → VBG, "-ed" → VBD/JJ, "-ly" → RB, "-tion" → NN
            f'suffix2={w[-2:]}':           1.0 if len(w) >= 2 else 0.0,
            f'suffix3={w[-3:]}':           1.0 if len(w) >= 3 else 0.0,
            f'suffix4={w[-4:]}':           1.0 if len(w) >= 4 else 0.0,

            # ── Morphological prefixes
            # "un-", "re-", "pre-" signal adjectives or verbs
            f'prefix2={w[:2].lower()}':    1.0 if len(w) >= 2 else 0.0,
            f'prefix3={w[:3].lower()}':    1.0 if len(w) >= 3 else 0.0,

            # ── Orthographic / word-shape features ──────────────────
            # Capitalised → likely NNP (proper noun) unless sentence-initial
            'is_capitalized':              1.0 if w[0].isupper() else 0.0,
            'is_all_caps':                 1.0 if w.isupper() else 0.0,
            # Digits → likely CD (cardinal number)
            'has_digit':                   1.0 if any(c.isdigit() for c in w) else 0.0,
            'is_digit':                    1.0 if w.isdigit() else 0.0,
            # Hyphen → often JJ (e.g. "well-known") or NN compound
            'has_hyphen':                  1.0 if '-' in w else 0.0,

            # ── Sequence context ────────────────────────────────────
            # MEMM's key advantage over HMM: we can condition on prev_tag
            # AND also on the surrounding words in a single model.
            f'prev_tag={prev_tag}':         1.0,
            f'prev_word={prev_word.lower()}': 1.0,
            f'next_word={next_word.lower()}': 1.0,

            # ── Interaction features (prev_tag × word shape) ────────
            # Helps when, e.g., a capitalised word after DT is likely NN
            f'prev_tag+cap={prev_tag}+{w[0].isupper()}': 1.0,

            # ── Unknown-word flag ────────────────────────────────────
            # Tells the model to rely more on morphology for OOV tokens
            'is_unknown':                  1.0 if w.lower() not in self.word_set else 0.0,
        }
        return features

    def train(self, tagged_sentences: List[List[Tuple[str, str]]],
              verbose: bool = False) -> None:
        """Fit the logistic regression model on extracted features.

        Args:
            tagged_sentences: List of [(word, tag), ...] sentences.
            verbose:          Print training summary if True.
        """
        # ── Pass 1: build vocabulary and tag set ─────────────────
        for sent in tagged_sentences:
            for word, tag in sent:
                self.word_set.add(word.lower())
                if tag not in self.tag_set:
                    self.tag_set.append(tag)

        # ── Pass 2: extract one feature dict per token ───────────
        X_dicts: List[Dict[str, float]] = []
        y:       List[str]             = []

        for sent in tagged_sentences:
            for i, (word, tag) in enumerate(sent):
                prev_tag  = sent[i - 1][1] if i > 0 else '<START>'
                prev_word = sent[i - 1][0] if i > 0 else '<s>'
                next_word = sent[i + 1][0] if i < len(sent) - 1 else '</s>'
                X_dicts.append(self.extract_features(word, prev_tag, prev_word, next_word))
                y.append(tag)

        # ── Convert dict features → sparse matrix ────────────────
        self.vectorizer = DictVectorizer(sparse=True)
        X = self.vectorizer.fit_transform(X_dicts)

        # ── Fit logistic regression ───────────────────────────────
        # solver='lbfgs' handles multi-class natively and handles
        # sparse input well. C controls L2 regularisation.
        self.model = LogisticRegression(
            max_iter=1000, C=self.C,
            solver='lbfgs', multi_class='auto'
        )
        self.model.fit(X, y)

        if verbose:
            n_features = len(self.vectorizer.get_feature_names_out())
            print(f"MEMM trained: {len(X_dicts)} tokens, {n_features} features, {len(self.tag_set)} tags")
            self.print_top_features()

    def predict(self, words: List[str],
                verbose: bool = False) -> List[str]:
        """Tag a sentence left-to-right (greedy decoding).

        Greedy: at each position we pick argmax P(tag | features).
        This is fast but susceptible to label-bias (see CRF section).
        Viterbi beam search would fix label-bias but at O(N²T) cost.

        Args:
            words:   Words to tag.
            verbose: Print per-word probability breakdowns.

        Returns:
            List of predicted POS tags.
        """
        tags:     List[str] = []
        prev_tag:       str = '<START>'

        for i, word in enumerate(words):
            prev_word = words[i - 1] if i > 0 else '<s>'
            next_word = words[i + 1] if i < len(words) - 1 else '</s>'
            feats     = self.extract_features(word, prev_tag, prev_word, next_word)
            X         = self.vectorizer.transform([feats])
            tag       = self.model.predict(X)[0]
            tags.append(tag)
            prev_tag  = tag  # feed predicted tag as context for next token

            if verbose:
                probs = self.model.predict_proba(X)[0]
                top3  = sorted(zip(self.model.classes_, probs),
                               key=lambda x: -x[1])[:3]
                top3_str = ', '.join(f'{t}={p:.2f}' for t, p in top3)
                print(f"  {word:15s} → {tag:6s}  [{top3_str}]")

        return tags

    def print_top_features(self, n: int = 10) -> None:
        """Print the n highest-magnitude feature weights per tag.

        Positive weights push toward the tag; negative weights push away.
        This is a key interpretability advantage of linear models.
        """
        feat_names = self.vectorizer.get_feature_names_out()
        for i, tag in enumerate(self.model.classes_):
            coefs = self.model.coef_[i]
            top_pos = np.argsort(coefs)[-n:][::-1]
            print(f"\n  Tag: {tag}")
            for idx in top_pos:
                print(f"    {feat_names[idx]:40s}  {coefs[idx]:+.3f}")

BiLSTM-CRF Tagger (PyTorch)

The BiLSTM-CRF combines two ideas: a bidirectional LSTM that reads the full sentence before tagging any word (fixing MEMM's left-to-right bias), and a CRF output layer that enforces globally consistent tag transitions (fixing MEMM's label-bias). This is the dominant neural baseline for sequence labeling.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim
from typing import List, Dict, Tuple


# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
#  Architecture Overview
#  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
#
#   Input sentence:   [w_1,  w_2,  ..., w_T]
#                          ↓ embedding lookup
#   Embeddings:       [e_1,  e_2,  ..., e_T]  each ∈ R^{embed_dim}
#                          ↓ BiLSTM (reads left→right AND right→left)
#   Context vectors:  [h_1,  h_2,  ..., h_T]  each ∈ R^{hidden_dim}
#                          ↓ linear projection
#   Emission scores:  [f_1,  f_2,  ..., f_T]  each ∈ R^{|tags|}
#                          ↓ CRF layer (global Viterbi decoding)
#   Tag sequence:     [t_1,  t_2,  ..., t_T]
#
#  BiLSTM advantage over left-to-right LSTM:
#    h_t = [h→_t ; h←_t]  — h←_t already saw the whole future.
#    This lets the model use "barks" to disambiguate "bank" earlier.
#
#  CRF advantage over softmax output:
#    Softmax tags each word independently → may produce "DT NN DT NN"
#    CRF learns transition scores (like HMM A-matrix) and finds the
#    globally best sequence, ruling out "DT DT DT".
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

class BiLSTMCRFTagger(nn.Module):
    """BiLSTM-CRF sequence labeler.

    Args:
        vocab_size:    Number of distinct words.
        tag_to_ix:     {"DT": 0, "NN": 1, ...}
        embedding_dim: Word embedding dimensionality (typically 100–300).
        hidden_dim:    Total BiLSTM output size; each direction gets
                       hidden_dim // 2 units (so concat = hidden_dim).
        dropout:       Dropout probability on LSTM output (regularisation).
    """

    START_TAG = "<START>"  # sentinel tag added to tag_to_ix
    STOP_TAG  = "<STOP>"

    def __init__(self,
                 vocab_size:    int,
                 tag_to_ix:    Dict[str, int],
                 embedding_dim: int = 100,
                 hidden_dim:    int = 128,
                 dropout:       float = 0.1):
        super().__init__()
        self.tag_to_ix    = tag_to_ix
        self.tagset_size  = len(tag_to_ix)
        self.hidden_dim   = hidden_dim

        # ── Layers ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
        # Lookup table: word index → dense embedding vector
        self.embedding = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, embedding_dim)

        # BiLSTM: processes the embedding sequence in both directions.
        # Each direction uses hidden_dim//2 units; outputs are concatenated.
        self.lstm = nn.LSTM(
            embedding_dim,
            hidden_dim // 2,
            num_layers=1,
            bidirectional=True,
            batch_first=True
        )
        self.dropout   = nn.Dropout(p=dropout)

        # Linear projection: hidden state → unnormalised tag scores (emissions)
        self.hidden2tag = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, self.tagset_size)

        # ── CRF transition matrix ────────────────────────────────────
        # transitions[i][j] = learned score of transitioning TO tag i FROM tag j
        # (note the transpose convention — row=destination, col=source)
        # Initialised with small random values; learnt jointly with LSTM.
        self.transitions = nn.Parameter(
            torch.randn(self.tagset_size, self.tagset_size) * 0.1
        )
        # Prevent the model from transitioning TO  or FROM 
        if self.START_TAG in tag_to_ix:
            self.transitions.data[:, tag_to_ix[self.START_TAG]] = -10000.0
        if self.STOP_TAG in tag_to_ix:
            self.transitions.data[tag_to_ix[self.STOP_TAG], :] = -10000.0

    def _get_emissions(self, sentence: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """Forward pass through embedding + BiLSTM → emission scores.

        Args:
            sentence: LongTensor of word indices, shape (1, seq_len).

        Returns:
            Emission scores, shape (seq_len, tagset_size).
            These are NOT probabilities — the CRF layer handles normalisation.
        """
        embeds   = self.embedding(sentence)          # (1, T, embed_dim)
        lstm_out, _ = self.lstm(embeds)              # (1, T, hidden_dim)
        lstm_out = self.dropout(lstm_out)
        emissions = self.hidden2tag(lstm_out.squeeze(0))  # (T, tagset_size)
        return emissions

    def _crf_forward_score(self, emissions: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """Compute log Z = log Σ_{all paths} exp(score(path)) for the CRF.

        This is the Forward algorithm applied to scores (not probabilities).
        Needed during training to normalise the negative log-likelihood.

        Uses the log-sum-exp trick to avoid numerical overflow.
        """
        # Initialise: all mass at START tag
        init_alphas = torch.full((self.tagset_size,), -10000.0)
        if self.START_TAG in self.tag_to_ix:
            init_alphas[self.tag_to_ix[self.START_TAG]] = 0.0
        else:
            init_alphas[0] = 0.0

        forward_var = init_alphas
        for emission in emissions:
            # For each next tag j: logsumexp over all previous tags i
            alphas_t = []
            for next_tag in range(self.tagset_size):
                emit_score = emission[next_tag].expand(self.tagset_size)
                trans_score = self.transitions[next_tag]
                next_tag_var = forward_var + trans_score + emit_score
                alphas_t.append(torch.logsumexp(next_tag_var, dim=0).unsqueeze(0))
            forward_var = torch.cat(alphas_t)
        # Sum out final state (or transition to STOP)
        terminal_var = forward_var
        if self.STOP_TAG in self.tag_to_ix:
            terminal_var = forward_var + self.transitions[self.tag_to_ix[self.STOP_TAG]]
        alpha = torch.logsumexp(terminal_var, dim=0)
        return alpha

    def _score_sentence(self, emissions: torch.Tensor,
                          tags: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """Score a specific tag sequence (numerator in CRF log-likelihood).

        score(O, T) = Σ_t [emissions[t][tag_t] + transitions[tag_t][tag_{t-1}]]
        """
        score = torch.zeros(1)
        if self.START_TAG in self.tag_to_ix:
            start = torch.tensor([self.tag_to_ix[self.START_TAG]], dtype=torch.long)
            tags = torch.cat([start, tags])

        for i, emission in enumerate(emissions):
            score += self.transitions[tags[i + 1], tags[i]] + emission[tags[i + 1]]
        if self.STOP_TAG in self.tag_to_ix:
            score += self.transitions[self.tag_to_ix[self.STOP_TAG], tags[-1]]
        return score

    def neg_log_likelihood(self, sentence: torch.Tensor,
                             tags: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """CRF negative log-likelihood loss for one training sentence.

        NLL = log Z - score(O, T_gold)
            = log(sum of all path scores) - score of correct path

        Minimising this pushes the correct path score up and all
        other path scores down, simultaneously — that's the CRF's
        global normalisation advantage over MEMM.
        """
        emissions  = self._get_emissions(sentence)
        forward_score = self._crf_forward_score(emissions)   # log Z
        gold_score    = self._score_sentence(emissions, tags) # score(O,T*)
        return forward_score - gold_score  # minimise this

    def _viterbi_decode(self, emissions: torch.Tensor) -> Tuple[float, List[int]]:
        """Find the globally best tag sequence via Viterbi on the CRF.

        This is structurally identical to the HMM Viterbi, but the
        "transition scores" and "emission scores" are learnt parameters
        rather than log-probability tables.

        Returns:
            (best_path_score, best_tag_sequence as list of indices)
        """
        backpointers: List[torch.Tensor] = []

        # Initialise from START tag
        init_vvars = torch.full((self.tagset_size,), -10000.0)
        if self.START_TAG in self.tag_to_ix:
            init_vvars[self.tag_to_ix[self.START_TAG]] = 0
        forward_var = init_vvars

        for emission in emissions:
            bptrs_t, viterbivars_t = [], []
            for next_tag in range(self.tagset_size):
                next_tag_var = forward_var + self.transitions[next_tag]
                best_tag_id  = next_tag_var.argmax()
                bptrs_t.append(best_tag_id)
                viterbivars_t.append(next_tag_var[best_tag_id].unsqueeze(0))
            forward_var = (torch.cat(viterbivars_t) + emission)
            backpointers.append(bptrs_t)

        # Transition to STOP tag (or just take best final state)
        if self.STOP_TAG in self.tag_to_ix:
            terminal_var = forward_var + self.transitions[self.tag_to_ix[self.STOP_TAG]]
        else:
            terminal_var = forward_var
        best_tag_id  = terminal_var.argmax().item()
        path_score   = terminal_var[best_tag_id]

        # Follow backpointers to reconstruct best path
        best_path = [best_tag_id]
        for bptrs_t in reversed(backpointers):
            best_tag_id = bptrs_t[best_tag_id].item()
            best_path.append(best_tag_id)
        if self.START_TAG in self.tag_to_ix:
            best_path.pop()  # remove the START tag
        best_path.reverse()
        return path_score.item(), best_path

    def forward(self, sentence: torch.Tensor) -> Tuple[float, List[int]]:
        """Predict tags at inference time (no gold tags needed).

        Args:
            sentence: LongTensor of word indices, shape (1, seq_len).

        Returns:
            (score, tag_indices)
        """
        emissions = self._get_emissions(sentence)
        return self._viterbi_decode(emissions)


# ── Training loop demo ────────────────────────────────────────────
def train_bilstm_crf(
    train_data: List[Tuple[List[str], List[str]]],
    n_epochs:   int = 10,
    lr:         float = 0.01
) -> BiLSTMCRFTagger:
    """Train a BiLSTM-CRF tagger on (sentence, tags) pairs.

    Args:
        train_data: List of (word_list, tag_list) tuples.
        n_epochs:   Training iterations over the full dataset.
        lr:         SGD learning rate.

    Returns:
        Trained BiLSTMCRFTagger model.
    """
    # Build vocabularies from training data
    word_to_ix: Dict[str, int] = {}
    tag_to_ix:  Dict[str, int] = {
        BiLSTMCRFTagger.START_TAG: 0,
        BiLSTMCRFTagger.STOP_TAG:  1
    }
    for words, tags in train_data:
        for w in words:
            if w not in word_to_ix:
                word_to_ix[w] = len(word_to_ix)
        for t in tags:
            if t not in tag_to_ix:
                tag_to_ix[t] = len(tag_to_ix)

    model     = BiLSTMCRFTagger(len(word_to_ix), tag_to_ix)
    optimizer = optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=lr, weight_decay=1e-4)

    ix_to_tag = {v: k for k, v in tag_to_ix.items()}

    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        total_loss = 0.0
        model.train()
        for words, tags in train_data:
            model.zero_grad()

            # Convert word strings → integer indices → LongTensor
            word_ids = torch.tensor(
                [word_to_ix[w] for w in words], dtype=torch.long
            ).unsqueeze(0)  # shape: (1, seq_len)

            # Convert gold tag strings → integer indices → LongTensor
            tag_ids = torch.tensor(
                [tag_to_ix[t] for t in tags], dtype=torch.long
            )

            # NLL loss = log Z - score(gold path)
            loss = model.neg_log_likelihood(word_ids, tag_ids)
            total_loss += loss.item()

            loss.backward()
            # Gradient clipping: prevents exploding gradients in RNNs
            torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=5.0)
            optimizer.step()

        if (epoch + 1) % 2 == 0:
            # Quick evaluation on training data to watch convergence
            model.eval()
            with torch.no_grad():
                correct = total = 0
                for words, gold_tags in train_data:
                    word_ids = torch.tensor(
                        [word_to_ix.get(w, 0) for w in words], dtype=torch.long
                    ).unsqueeze(0)
                    _, pred_ids = model(word_ids)
                    pred_tags = [ix_to_tag[i] for i in pred_ids]
                    correct += sum(g == p for g, p in zip(gold_tags, pred_tags))
                    total   += len(gold_tags)
            print(f"  Epoch {epoch+1:2d}: loss={total_loss:.3f}  train_acc={correct/total:.2%}")

    return model

if __name__ == "__main__":
    training_data = [
        (["The",  "dog",  "chased", "the", "cat"],
         ["DT",   "NN",   "VBD",    "DT",  "NN"]),
        (["She",  "ran",  "quickly"],
         ["PRP",  "VBD", "RB"]),
        (["The",  "big",  "dog",     "sat"],
         ["DT",   "JJ",   "NN",      "VBD"]),
    ]

    print("── Training BiLSTM-CRF ────────────────────────────────────")
    model = train_bilstm_crf(training_data, n_epochs=10, lr=0.01)

    # Rebuild word_to_ix for inference (in practice store it with the model)
    word_to_ix: Dict[str, int] = {}
    tag_to_ix   = {BiLSTMCRFTagger.START_TAG: 0, BiLSTMCRFTagger.STOP_TAG: 1}
    for words, tags in training_data:
        for w in words:
            word_to_ix.setdefault(w, len(word_to_ix))
        for t in tags:
            tag_to_ix.setdefault(t, len(tag_to_ix))
    ix_to_tag = {v: k for k, v in tag_to_ix.items()}

    print("\n── Inference: 'The dog sat' ───────────────────────────────")
    model.eval()
    with torch.no_grad():
        test_words = ["The", "dog", "sat"]
        ids = torch.tensor(
            [word_to_ix.get(w, 0) for w in test_words], dtype=torch.long
        ).unsqueeze(0)
        score, tag_ids = model(ids)
        predicted = [ix_to_tag[i] for i in tag_ids]
        for w, t in zip(test_words, predicted):
            print(f"  {w:12s} → {t}")
        print(f"  Path score: {score:.4f}")
