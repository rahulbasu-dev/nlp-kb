POS Tagging, Markov Chains & HMMs

Part-of-speech tagging assigns grammatical labels to words in a sentence. Hidden Markov Models provide an elegant probabilistic framework for this fundamental NLP task.

Sequence Labeling with Probabilistic Models

💡 Intuition

Why labeling words with their grammatical roles matters

What is Part-of-Speech Tagging?

Part-of-speech (POS) tagging is the task of assigning a grammatical category—such as noun, verb, adjective, or preposition—to each word in a sentence. It is one of the oldest and most fundamental tasks in Natural Language Processing.

Given a sentence like "The cat sat on the mat", a POS tagger would produce:

The /DT cat /NN sat /VBD on /IN the /DT mat /NN

Why Does POS Tagging Matter?

  • Parsing: Knowing that a word is a noun vs. a verb is essential for building parse trees.
  • Named Entity Recognition: Proper nouns (NNP) are strong signals for entity detection.
  • Information Retrieval: Filtering by POS improves search relevance.
  • Machine Translation: Word order and agreement rules depend on parts of speech.
  • Text-to-Speech: Pronunciation of words like "record" depends on whether it is a noun or verb.

The Ambiguity Problem

Many English words can serve as multiple parts of speech. This lexical ambiguity is the core challenge:

  • "book" — Noun: "I read a book." vs. Verb: "Please book the flight."
  • "can" — Modal verb: "I can swim." vs. Noun: "Open the can."
  • "run" — Verb: "I run daily." vs. Noun: "a run in the park."
  • "back" — Noun, verb, adjective, or adverb depending on context.

🔍 The Context Clues Analogy

Imagine you hear the word "bank" in isolation—you cannot tell if it means a financial institution or a river bank. But in context, "I deposited money at the bank," the surrounding words resolve the ambiguity instantly. POS taggers work the same way: they use context—especially the tags of neighboring words—to disambiguate.

✏️ Try It Yourself!

Consider the sentence: "The fish can swim."

How many words are ambiguous (could be more than one POS)? Which ones, and what are the possible POS tags?

📦 Word Classes

Open and closed class categories in English

Open Classes

Open classes are productive—new words are regularly added to the language.

Nouns (NN)

People, places, things, concepts. The largest open class.

Examples: dog, city, freedom, algorithm, blockchain

Verbs (VB)

Actions, states, occurrences. Inflect for tense, aspect, mood.

Examples: run, think, optimize, containerize

Adjectives (JJ)

Modify nouns, describing properties.

Examples: big, red, computational, scalable

Adverbs (RB)

Modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs.

Examples: quickly, very, efficiently, surprisingly

Closed Classes

Closed classes have a fixed membership—new words are rarely added.

Determiners (DT)

Introduce noun phrases: the, a, an, this, that, every

Pronouns (PRP)

Stand in for nouns: I, you, he, she, it, they, we

Prepositions (IN)

Express spatial or temporal relations: in, on, at, by, with, from

Conjunctions (CC)

Connect words or clauses: and, but, or, nor, for, yet, so

Modals (MD)

Auxiliary verbs expressing possibility, necessity: can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must

Particles (RP)

Small words in phrasal verbs: up in "give up", off in "turn off"

🔗 Markov Chains

A foundation for sequential probabilistic modeling

States, Transitions, and the Memoryless Property

A Markov chain is a stochastic model describing a sequence of states where the probability of transitioning to the next state depends only on the current state—not on the sequence of states that preceded it.

Formally, a Markov chain consists of:

  • A set of states \( S = \{s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N\} \)
  • A transition probability matrix \( A \) where \( a_{ij} = P(q_t = s_j \mid q_{t-1} = s_i) \)
  • An initial probability distribution \( \pi \) where \( \pi_i = P(q_1 = s_i) \)
The Markov Property (Memorylessness)
\[ P(q_t \mid q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_{t-1}) = P(q_t \mid q_{t-1}) \]

The future state depends only on the present state, not on the entire history. This dramatically simplifies computation.

Transition Probability
\[ a_{ij} = P(q_t = s_j \mid q_{t-1} = s_i), \quad \sum_{j=1}^{N} a_{ij} = 1 \;\;\forall\; i \]

Each row of the transition matrix sums to 1, since from any state we must go somewhere.

Sequence Probability
\[ P(q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_T) = \pi_{q_1} \cdot \prod_{t=2}^{T} a_{q_{t-1}, q_t} \]

The probability of an entire sequence is the initial probability times the product of all transition probabilities along the path.

Example: Weather Markov Chain

Consider a simple weather model with two states: Sunny and Rainy.

From \ ToSunnyRainy
Sunny0.70.3
Rainy0.40.6

If today is Sunny, there is a 70% chance tomorrow is Sunny and 30% chance of Rain. If today is Rainy, there is a 40% chance of Sunny and 60% chance of Rain tomorrow.

🔑 Key Insight

Markov chains model observable sequences. In POS tagging, the tags form a Markov chain (the tag of the current word depends on the previous tag), but we do not directly observe the tags—we observe the words. This is why we need Hidden Markov Models.

🎭 Hidden Markov Models

When states are hidden and only observations are visible

Hidden States vs. Observations

In a Hidden Markov Model (HMM), the system's states are not directly observable. Instead, each state produces an observation (also called an emission) according to a probability distribution. We observe the emissions and must infer the hidden state sequence.

HMM Components: \( \lambda = (A, B, \pi) \)

  • \( A \) — Transition probability matrix: \( a_{ij} = P(q_t = j \mid q_{t-1} = i) \)
  • \( B \) — Emission probability matrix: \( b_j(o_t) = P(o_t \mid q_t = j) \)
  • \( \pi \) — Initial state distribution: \( \pi_j = P(q_1 = j) \)
Emission Probability
\[ b_j(o_t) = P(o_t \mid q_t = j) \]

The probability that state \( j \) emits observation \( o_t \). Each state has its own probability distribution over possible observations.

Joint Probability of Observations and States
\[ P(O, Q \mid \lambda) = \pi_{q_1} \cdot b_{q_1}(o_1) \cdot \prod_{t=2}^{T} a_{q_{t-1}, q_t} \cdot b_{q_t}(o_t) \]

The joint probability of observing sequence \( O = o_1, \ldots, o_T \) and state sequence \( Q = q_1, \ldots, q_T \) under model \( \lambda \).

Imagine you are a climatologist in the year 2100 trying to figure out past weather from someone's diary that only records how many ice creams they ate each day (1, 2, or 3). The weather (Hot or Cold) is the hidden state; the ice cream count is the observation.

Three Fundamental HMM Problems

  1. Evaluation (Likelihood): Given a model \( \lambda \) and observation sequence \( O \), compute \( P(O \mid \lambda) \). Solved by the Forward algorithm.
  2. Decoding: Given a model \( \lambda \) and observation sequence \( O \), find the most likely state sequence \( Q^* \). Solved by the Viterbi algorithm.
  3. Learning: Given an observation sequence \( O \), find model parameters \( \lambda \) that maximize \( P(O \mid \lambda) \). Solved by the Baum-Welch algorithm (a special case of EM).

➡️ The Forward Algorithm

Computing the total probability of an observation sequence

What Problem Does It Solve?

Given an HMM \( \lambda = (A, B, \pi) \) and an observation sequence \( O = (o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_T) \), the Forward algorithm answers:

What is \( P(O \mid \lambda) \)? — How well does this HMM explain what we observed?

This is useful for:

  • Model comparison: Given two trained HMMs, which better explains an observed word sequence?
  • Sequence scoring: How likely is this particular sentence under our POS model?
  • EM training (Baum-Welch): The E-step requires \( P(O \mid \lambda) \) as a normaliser.
Cross-reference: For a detailed numerical walkthrough using the ice-cream HMM, see the POS Models page → Forward Algorithm section. This section focuses on the POS tagging perspective with "I saw the dog".

Why Not Brute Force?

A naive approach enumerates every possible state sequence:

  • With \( N \) tags and a sentence of \( T \) words, there are \( N^T \) possible tag sequences.
  • Penn Treebank has 45 tags. For a 10-word sentence: \( 45^{10} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{16} \) sequences — infeasible.

The Forward algorithm reduces this to \( O(N^2 T) \) using dynamic programming, by reusing partial sums across time steps.

The Forward Variable

Forward Variable Definition
\[ \alpha_t(j) = P(o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_t,\; q_t = j \mid \lambda) \]

The probability of observing the first \( t \) words and being in state \( j \) at time \( t \).

Initialization
\[ \alpha_1(j) = \pi_j \cdot b_j(o_1) \]

Initial state probability times emission probability for the first word.

Recursion
\[ \alpha_t(j) = \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \right] \cdot b_j(o_t) \]

Sum over all possible previous states \( i \), weight each by the transition \( a_{ij} \), then multiply by the emission \( b_j(o_t) \).

Termination
\[ P(O \mid \lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_T(j) \]

Sum the forward values at the final time step over all states.

Worked Example: "I saw" (2 states)

Using only PRP and VBD with the same probability tables as the Viterbi section below:

Initialization (t=1, word = "I"):

\( \alpha_1(\text{PRP}) = \pi_{\text{PRP}} \cdot b_{\text{PRP}}(\text{I}) = 0.40 \times 0.50 = 0.200 \)
\( \alpha_1(\text{VBD}) = \pi_{\text{VBD}} \cdot b_{\text{VBD}}(\text{I}) = 0.05 \times 0.01 = 0.0005 \)

Recursion (t=2, word = "saw") — SUM over predecessors:

\( \alpha_2(\text{VBD}) = [\alpha_1(\text{PRP}) \cdot a_{\text{PRP} \to \text{VBD}} + \alpha_1(\text{VBD}) \cdot a_{\text{VBD} \to \text{VBD}}] \cdot b_{\text{VBD}}(\text{saw}) \)
\( \phantom{\alpha_2(\text{VBD})} = [0.200 \times 0.50 + 0.0005 \times 0.01] \times 0.40 \)
\( \phantom{\alpha_2(\text{VBD})} = [0.1000 + 0.000005] \times 0.40 \approx 0.040 \)

\( \alpha_2(\text{PRP}) = [\alpha_1(\text{PRP}) \cdot a_{\text{PRP} \to \text{PRP}} + \alpha_1(\text{VBD}) \cdot a_{\text{VBD} \to \text{PRP}}] \cdot b_{\text{PRP}}(\text{saw}) \)
\( \phantom{\alpha_2(\text{PRP})} = [0.200 \times 0.01 + 0.0005 \times 0.10] \times 0.01 \)
\( \phantom{\alpha_2(\text{PRP})} = [0.002 + 0.00005] \times 0.01 \approx 2.05 \times 10^{-5} \)

Termination: \( P(\text{"I saw"} \mid \lambda) = \alpha_2(\text{PRP}) + \alpha_2(\text{VBD}) \approx 0.040 \)

Notice \( \alpha_2(\text{VBD}) \) dominates because the PRP→VBD transition is strong (0.50) and VBD emits "saw" well (0.40).

🧮 HMM POS Tagging & Viterbi

Mapping the HMM framework to sequence labeling

HMM for POS Tagging

In POS tagging, the HMM mapping is:

  • Hidden states = POS tags (NN, VB, DT, JJ, ...)
  • Observations = Words in the sentence
  • Transition probabilities \( A \): How likely is tag \( t_j \) after tag \( t_i \)?
  • Emission probabilities \( B \): How likely is word \( w \) given tag \( t \)?

The goal is to find the tag sequence \( T^* \) that maximizes:

Most Likely Tag Sequence
\[ T^* = \arg\max_{T} P(T \mid W) = \arg\max_{T} P(W \mid T) \cdot P(T) \]

By Bayes' rule, we find the tag sequence that maximizes the product of the likelihood \( P(W|T) \) and the prior \( P(T) \).

MLE Training from Tagged Corpora

Given a tagged training corpus, we estimate transition and emission probabilities using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE):

Transition Probability (MLE)
\[ P(t_i \mid t_{i-1}) = \frac{C(t_{i-1}, t_i)}{C(t_{i-1})} \]

Count how often tag \( t_i \) follows tag \( t_{i-1} \), divided by the total count of \( t_{i-1} \).

Emission Probability (MLE)
\[ P(w_i \mid t_i) = \frac{C(t_i, w_i)}{C(t_i)} \]

Count how often word \( w_i \) appears with tag \( t_i \), divided by the total count of tag \( t_i \).

The Viterbi Algorithm

The Viterbi algorithm is a dynamic programming algorithm that finds the most likely sequence of hidden states. It avoids the exponential cost of evaluating all possible tag sequences.

Forward vs Viterbi — one key difference:

The Forward algorithm SUMS over all paths: \( \alpha_t(j) = \left[\sum_i \alpha_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij}\right] \cdot b_j(o_t) \)
The Viterbi algorithm takes MAX over all paths: \( v_t(j) = \max_i\left[ v_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij}\right] \cdot b_j(o_t) \)

Both run in \( O(N^2 T) \) — only the aggregation operator differs. Forward answers "what is the total probability of this sequence?"; Viterbi answers "what is the single best tag sequence?".
Viterbi Initialization
\[ v_1(j) = \pi_j \cdot b_j(o_1) \]

For each state \( j \), the initial Viterbi value is the initial probability of \( j \) times the probability that \( j \) emits the first observation.

Viterbi Recursion
\[ v_t(j) = \max_{i=1}^{N} \left[ v_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \cdot b_j(o_t) \right] \]

At each time step, for each state \( j \), we find the maximum probability path ending at \( j \) by considering all possible previous states \( i \).

Backpointer
\[ bp_t(j) = \arg\max_{i=1}^{N} \left[ v_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \cdot b_j(o_t) \right] \]

The backpointer records which previous state \( i \) gave the maximum value, allowing us to trace back the best path.

Log-space Viterbi (avoids underflow):

Multiplying many small probabilities together causes numerical underflow. The solution: work in log-space. \[ \log v_t(j) = \max_{i}\left[ \log v_{t-1}(i) + \log a_{ij} + \log b_j(o_t) \right] \] Products become sums, and \(\max\) is preserved under the monotone log transform.

Example: \( 0.200 \times 0.50 \times 0.40 = 0.04 \) vs. \( \ln(0.200) + \ln(0.50) + \ln(0.40) = -1.609 + (-0.693) + (-0.916) = -3.218 \) and \( e^{-3.218} \approx 0.04 \) ✓

Using the same probabilities above, what would \( v_2(\text{PRP}) \) be for the word "saw"? Why is this value so small?

This is extremely small because (1) "saw" has very low emission probability from PRP (0.01), and (2) transitions into PRP from most states are low. The model correctly disfavors tagging "saw" as a pronoun.

🔄 Baum-Welch: Unsupervised HMM Learning

Training an HMM without a labelled corpus using Expectation-Maximisation

Motivation

The MLE training above (Section 6) requires a tagged corpus. But for many languages and domains, labelled data is expensive or unavailable. Baum-Welch lets us train an HMM from raw (untagged) text.

Analogy: Like a chef adjusting a recipe by repeatedly tasting the dish — no recipe card needed. Each iteration improves the model until the flavour (likelihood) stops improving.

Baum-Welch is an instance of the Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm applied to HMMs:

  • E-step: Given the current model \(\lambda\), compute the expected tag assignments for each word position.
  • M-step: Given the expected assignments, re-estimate \(A\), \(B\), \(\pi\) to maximise likelihood.
  • Repeat until \(P(O \mid \lambda)\) stops increasing.

The Backward Variable \(\beta\)

The Forward algorithm \(\alpha_t(j)\) accumulates evidence from the left. For EM we also need evidence from the right:

Backward Variable Definition
\[ \beta_t(i) = P(o_{t+1}, o_{t+2}, \ldots, o_T \mid q_t = i,\; \lambda) \]

Probability of all future observations given we are in state \(i\) at time \(t\).

Initialization
\[ \beta_T(i) = 1 \quad \text{for all } i \]

At the final time step there are no future observations, so the probability is 1.

Recursion (backward)
\[ \beta_t(i) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} a_{ij} \cdot b_j(o_{t+1}) \cdot \beta_{t+1}(j) \]

Sum over all states \(j\) we could transition into: transition probability × emission × future backward value.

\(\alpha\) looks forward; \(\beta\) looks backward. Together they bracket every state at every time step.

The \(\gamma\) and \(\xi\) Variables

Combining \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) gives us the two sufficient statistics for the M-step:

\(\gamma_t(i)\) — State occupancy probability
\[ \gamma_t(i) = \frac{\alpha_t(i) \cdot \beta_t(i)}{P(O \mid \lambda)} \]

Probability of being in state \(i\) at time \(t\), given the entire observation sequence.

\(\xi_t(i,j)\) — Transition occupancy probability
\[ \xi_t(i,j) = \frac{\alpha_t(i) \cdot a_{ij} \cdot b_j(o_{t+1}) \cdot \beta_{t+1}(j)}{P(O \mid \lambda)} \]

Expected number of times we transition from state \(i\) to state \(j\) at time \(t\).

M-step: Re-estimation Formulas

Re-estimate initial probabilities
\[ \hat{\pi}_i = \gamma_1(i) \]

The expected probability of starting in state \(i\).

Re-estimate transition probabilities
\[ \hat{a}_{ij} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \xi_t(i,j)}{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \gamma_t(i)} \]

Expected transitions \(i \to j\) divided by expected time spent in state \(i\).

Re-estimate emission probabilities
\[ \hat{b}_j(v_k) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t:\, o_t = v_k} \gamma_t(j)}{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} \gamma_t(j)} \]

Expected time in state \(j\) while emitting symbol \(v_k\), divided by total expected time in \(j\).

Worked Example: Backward Pass ("I saw", PRP/VBD)

Using the same 2-state simplified model from the Forward example above (T=2, states = PRP, VBD):

Initialization (t=T=2):

\( \beta_2(\text{PRP}) = 1 \)
\( \beta_2(\text{VBD}) = 1 \)

Recursion (t=1):

\( \beta_1(\text{PRP}) = a_{\text{PRP} \to \text{PRP}} \cdot b_{\text{PRP}}(\text{saw}) \cdot \beta_2(\text{PRP}) + a_{\text{PRP} \to \text{VBD}} \cdot b_{\text{VBD}}(\text{saw}) \cdot \beta_2(\text{VBD}) \)
\( \phantom{\beta_1(\text{PRP})} = 0.01 \times 0.01 \times 1 + 0.50 \times 0.40 \times 1 = 0.0001 + 0.200 = 0.2001 \)

\( \beta_1(\text{VBD}) = a_{\text{VBD} \to \text{PRP}} \cdot b_{\text{PRP}}(\text{saw}) \cdot 1 + a_{\text{VBD} \to \text{VBD}} \cdot b_{\text{VBD}}(\text{saw}) \cdot 1 \)
\( \phantom{\beta_1(\text{VBD})} = 0.10 \times 0.01 + 0.01 \times 0.40 = 0.001 + 0.004 = 0.005 \)

State occupancy \(\gamma\) at t=1: Recall \(\alpha_1(\text{PRP}) = 0.200\), \(\alpha_1(\text{VBD}) = 0.0005\), \(P(O \mid \lambda) \approx 0.04\).

\( \gamma_1(\text{PRP}) = \frac{0.200 \times 0.2001}{0.04} \approx 1.0 \) → Almost certainly PRP at t=1 ✓
\( \gamma_1(\text{VBD}) = \frac{0.0005 \times 0.005}{0.04} \approx 6 \times 10^{-5} \) → Almost never VBD at t=1 ✓

These \(\gamma\) values confirm that "I" is overwhelmingly a PRP, which the M-step would reinforce in the next iteration.

🐍 Python Implementation

A complete HMM POS tagger in Python — with Forward, Viterbi, trellis printing, and Baum-Welch

What this code implements

The class below mirrors exactly the math from Sections 5–8:

  • train() — MLE from a tagged corpus (Section 6), add-1 (Laplace) smoothing
  • forward() — computes P(O|λ) via α, prints a step-by-step trellis (Section 6a)
  • viterbi() — log-space decoding with backpointer trace, prints the trellis (Section 6b)
  • baum_welch() — unsupervised re-estimation from raw text using α, β, γ, ξ (Section 6c)
  • evaluate() — token accuracy + per-tag confusion breakdown

from collections import defaultdict, Counter
from typing import List, Tuple, Dict, Optional
import math


# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
#  HMMPosTagger
#  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
#  A first-order Hidden Markov Model for part-of-speech tagging.
#
#  Model parameters (λ = A, B, π):
#    π[tag]          = P(tag | sentence start)
#    A[prev][tag]    = P(tag | prev)         ← transition matrix
#    B[tag][word]    = P(word | tag)         ← emission matrix
#
#  Three tasks (Rabiner 1989):
#    1. Evaluation  → forward()       — P(O | λ)
#    2. Decoding    → viterbi()       — argmax_T P(T | W, λ)
#    3. Learning    → baum_welch()    — argmax_λ P(O | λ)  (unsupervised)
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
class HMMPosTagger:
    """Hidden Markov Model POS Tagger.

    Implements all three HMM tasks:
      - Supervised MLE training from a tagged corpus
      - Forward algorithm: P(O | λ)
      - Viterbi decoding: best tag sequence (log-space)
      - Baum-Welch EM: unsupervised re-estimation from raw text

    Smoothing: add-1 (Laplace) throughout to handle unseen events.
    """

    def __init__(self) -> None:
        # Observed vocabulary and tag set (populated during training)
        self.tags: set = set()
        self.vocab: set = set()

        # Raw co-occurrence counts from the training corpus
        self.transition_counts: Dict[str, Counter] = defaultdict(Counter)
        self.emission_counts:   Dict[str, Counter] = defaultdict(Counter)
        self.tag_counts:        Counter = Counter()
        self.initial_counts:    Counter = Counter()

        # Smoothed probability tables (filled by _compute_* helpers)
        self.transition_probs: Dict[str, Dict[str, float]] = {}
        self.emission_probs:   Dict[str, Dict[str, float]] = {}
        self.initial_probs:    Dict[str, float] = {}

    # ── Training ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    def train(self, tagged_sentences: List[List[Tuple[str, str]]],
              verbose: bool = False) -> None:
        """Estimate λ = (A, B, π) via MLE with add-1 smoothing.

        Args:
            tagged_sentences: List of sentences; each sentence is a list of
                              (word, tag) pairs, e.g. [("The","DT"), ...].
            verbose: If True, print corpus statistics after training.

        Algorithm (one pass over the corpus):
          1. For sentence-initial words, increment initial_counts[tag].
          2. For every consecutive (prev_tag, tag) pair, increment
             transition_counts[prev_tag][tag].
          3. For every (tag, word) pair, increment emission_counts[tag][word].
          Then convert raw counts to smoothed probabilities.
        """
        for sentence in tagged_sentences:
            if not sentence:
                continue

            # ── sentence-initial token ──────────────────────────────
            word0, tag0 = sentence[0]
            self.initial_counts[tag0] += 1
            self.emission_counts[tag0][word0] += 1
            self.tag_counts[tag0] += 1
            self.tags.add(tag0)
            self.vocab.add(word0)

            # ── remaining tokens ────────────────────────────────────
            for i in range(1, len(sentence)):
                word, tag = sentence[i]
                prev_tag  = sentence[i - 1][1]
                self.transition_counts[prev_tag][tag] += 1
                self.emission_counts[tag][word] += 1
                self.tag_counts[tag] += 1
                self.tags.add(tag)
                self.vocab.add(word)

        self._compute_transition_probs()
        self._compute_emission_probs()
        self._compute_initial_probs(len(tagged_sentences))

        if verbose:
            print(f"Training complete.")
            print(f"  Tags ({len(self.tags)}): {sorted(self.tags)}")
            print(f"  Vocabulary size: {len(self.vocab)}")
            print(f"  Sentences: {len(tagged_sentences)}")
            print("\n  Top initial probabilities:")
            for tag, p in sorted(self.initial_probs.items(),
                                     key=lambda x: -x[1])[:5]:
                print(f"    π({tag}) = {p:.4f}")

    def _compute_transition_probs(self) -> None:
        """A[prev][tag] = (C(prev→tag) + 1) / (C(prev) + |tags|)
        Add-1 smoothing: every transition gets at least 1 pseudo-count,
        preventing zero probabilities for unseen bigrams."""
        n_tags = len(self.tags)
        for prev_tag in self.tags:
            self.transition_probs[prev_tag] = {}
            total = sum(self.transition_counts[prev_tag].values()) + n_tags
            for tag in self.tags:
                count = self.transition_counts[prev_tag].get(tag, 0) + 1
                self.transition_probs[prev_tag][tag] = count / total

    def _compute_emission_probs(self) -> None:
        """B[tag][word] = (C(tag,word) + 1) / (C(tag) + |vocab|)
        Add-1 smoothing ensures every (tag, word) pair has non-zero
        probability, which matters for unknown words at test time."""
        v_size = len(self.vocab)
        for tag in self.tags:
            self.emission_probs[tag] = {}
            total = self.tag_counts[tag] + v_size
            for word in self.vocab:
                count = self.emission_counts[tag].get(word, 0) + 1
                self.emission_probs[tag][word] = count / total

    def _compute_initial_probs(self, n_sentences: int) -> None:
        """π[tag] = (C(sentence starts with tag) + 1) / (n_sentences + |tags|)"""
        n_tags = len(self.tags)
        for tag in self.tags:
            self.initial_probs[tag] = (
                self.initial_counts.get(tag, 0) + 1
            ) / (n_sentences + n_tags)

    def _emission(self, tag: str, word: str) -> float:
        """Return B[tag][word], falling back to 1/|vocab| for unknown words.

        Unknown word heuristic: treat the word as equally likely to have
        been emitted by any tag (uniform distribution over vocab size).
        In production systems you would use morphological features here.
        """
        if word in self.vocab:
            return self.emission_probs[tag].get(word, 1e-10)
        return 1.0 / max(len(self.vocab), 1)

    # ── Task 1: Evaluation (Forward Algorithm) ──────────────────────

    def forward(self, sentence: List[str],
               verbose: bool = False) -> Tuple[float, List[Dict[str, float]]]:
        """Compute P(O | λ) using the Forward algorithm.

        α_t(j) = P(o_1…o_t, q_t=j | λ)

        Initialization:  α_1(j) = π_j · B[j][o_1]
        Recursion:       α_t(j) = [Σ_i α_{t-1}(i) · A[i][j]] · B[j][o_t]
        Termination:     P(O|λ) = Σ_j α_T(j)

        Args:
            sentence: List of observed words.
            verbose:  If True, print the full alpha trellis.

        Returns:
            (total_probability, alpha_table)
            alpha_table[t] = dict mapping tag → α_t(tag).
        """
        tags = list(self.tags)
        T    = len(sentence)
        alpha: List[Dict[str, float]] = []

        # ── Initialization (t = 0) ──────────────────────────────────
        a0 = {}
        for j in tags:
            # α_1(j) = π_j · B[j][o_1]
            a0[j] = self.initial_probs[j] * self._emission(j, sentence[0])
        alpha.append(a0)

        # ── Recursion (t = 1 … T-1) ─────────────────────────────────
        for t in range(1, T):
            at = {}
            for j in tags:
                # Sum over all possible previous states i
                incoming = sum(
                    alpha[t - 1][i] * self.transition_probs[i][j]
                    for i in tags
                )
                # Multiply by emission probability for current word
                at[j] = incoming * self._emission(j, sentence[t])
            alpha.append(at)

        # ── Termination ─────────────────────────────────────────────
        total = sum(alpha[T - 1][j] for j in tags)

        if verbose:
            self._print_trellis(sentence, tags, alpha, label="Forward (α)")
            print(f"\n  P(O | λ) = Σ_j α_T(j) = {total:.6e}\n")

        return total, alpha

    # ── Task 2: Decoding (Viterbi Algorithm) ────────────────────────

    def viterbi(self, sentence: List[str],
               verbose: bool = False) -> List[str]:
        """Find the most likely tag sequence using Viterbi decoding.

        Works in log-space to avoid floating-point underflow when
        multiplying many small probabilities together.

        v_t(j) = max_i [ v_{t-1}(i) + log A[i][j] ] + log B[j][o_t]

        vs. Forward which uses SUM instead of MAX.

        Args:
            sentence: List of words.
            verbose:  If True, print the Viterbi trellis + backtrace.

        Returns:
            List of POS tags, one per word.
        """
        if not sentence:
            return []

        tags = list(self.tags)
        T    = len(sentence)
        LOG_ZERO = float('-inf')

        # viterbi[t][tag] = best log-probability of any path ending at (t, tag)
        vt = [{} for _ in range(T)]
        # bp[t][tag] = which tag at t-1 gave that best score
        bp = [{} for _ in range(T)]

        # ── Initialization ──────────────────────────────────────────
        for j in tags:
            emit = self._emission(j, sentence[0])
            # log(π_j) + log(B[j][o_1])
            vt[0][j] = math.log(self.initial_probs[j]) + math.log(emit)
            bp[0][j] = None  # no predecessor at t=0

        # ── Recursion ───────────────────────────────────────────────
        for t in range(1, T):
            for j in tags:
                log_emit = math.log(self._emission(j, sentence[t]))
                best_score, best_prev = LOG_ZERO, None
                for i in tags:
                    # Score of best path through i, then transitioning to j
                    score = (vt[t - 1][i]
                             + math.log(self.transition_probs[i][j])
                             + log_emit)
                    if score > best_score:
                        best_score = score
                        best_prev  = i
                vt[t][j] = best_score
                bp[t][j] = best_prev

        # ── Termination ─────────────────────────────────────────────
        best_final = max(tags, key=lambda j: vt[T - 1][j])

        # ── Backtrace ───────────────────────────────────────────────
        path = [best_final]
        for t in range(T - 1, 0, -1):
            path.append(bp[t][path[-1]])
        path.reverse()

        if verbose:
            # Show trellis with exp() values for readability
            vt_prob = [
                {j: math.exp(vt[t][j]) for j in tags} for t in range(T)
            ]
            self._print_trellis(sentence, tags, vt_prob, label="Viterbi (v)")
            print("\n  Best path (backtrace):")
            for word, tag in zip(sentence, path):
                print(f"    {word:12s} → {tag}")

        return path

    # ── Task 3: Learning (Baum-Welch EM) ────────────────────────────

    def baum_welch(self, raw_sentences: List[List[str]],
                   n_iter: int = 10,
                   tol: float = 1e-6,
                   verbose: bool = True) -> List[float]:
        """Unsupervised HMM parameter estimation via Baum-Welch (EM).

        Requires a trained (or randomly initialised) model as a starting
        point. Iteratively updates A, B, π so that P(O | λ) increases.

        E-step: compute α (forward), β (backward), γ (state occupancy),
                ξ (transition occupancy) for each sentence.
        M-step: re-estimate A, B, π from the expected counts.

        Args:
            raw_sentences: List of sentences; each sentence is a list of
                          plain words (no tags).
            n_iter:       Maximum EM iterations.
            tol:          Stop early if log-likelihood improves by less than
                          this amount between iterations.
            verbose:      Print log-likelihood after each iteration.

        Returns:
            List of per-iteration log-likelihood values (for convergence plot).
        """
        tags       = list(self.tags)
        N          = len(tags)
        tag_idx    = {t: i for i, t in enumerate(tags)}
        log_likes  = []
        prev_ll    = float('-inf')

        for iteration in range(n_iter):

            # ── E-step accumulators ─────────────────────────────────
            # Expected counts (soft versions of the hard counts in train())
            gamma_init  = Counter()          # expected initial counts
            gamma_total = Counter()          # expected time in each state
            xi_counts   = defaultdict(Counter)  # expected transitions
            emit_counts = defaultdict(Counter)  # expected emissions
            total_ll    = 0.0

            for sentence in raw_sentences:
                T = len(sentence)
                if T == 0:
                    continue

                # Forward pass: α_t(j) = P(o_1..o_t, q_t=j)
                p_obs, alpha = self.forward(sentence)
                if p_obs == 0:
                    continue
                total_ll += math.log(p_obs)

                # Backward pass: β_t(j) = P(o_{t+1}..o_T | q_t=j)
                beta = self._backward(sentence)

                # γ_t(j) = α_t(j) · β_t(j) / P(O|λ)
                for t in range(T):
                    for j in tags:
                        g = alpha[t][j] * beta[t][j] / p_obs
                        gamma_total[j] += g
                        emit_counts[j][sentence[t]] += g
                        if t == 0:
                            gamma_init[j] += g

                # ξ_t(i,j) = α_t(i)·A[i][j]·B[j][o_{t+1}]·β_{t+1}(j) / P(O|λ)
                for t in range(T - 1):
                    denom = sum(
                        alpha[t][i] * self.transition_probs[i][j]
                        * self._emission(j, sentence[t + 1])
                        * beta[t + 1][j]
                        for i in tags for j in tags
                    )
                    if denom == 0:
                        continue
                    for i in tags:
                        for j in tags:
                            xi = (alpha[t][i]
                                  * self.transition_probs[i][j]
                                  * self._emission(j, sentence[t + 1])
                                  * beta[t + 1][j]) / denom
                            xi_counts[i][j] += xi

            log_likes.append(total_ll)

            # ── M-step: re-estimate π, A, B ─────────────────────────
            total_init = sum(gamma_init.values()) or 1.0
            for j in tags:
                self.initial_probs[j] = gamma_init[j] / total_init

            for i in tags:
                denom = sum(xi_counts[i].values()) or 1.0
                for j in tags:
                    self.transition_probs[i][j] = xi_counts[i].get(j, 0) / denom

            for j in tags:
                denom = gamma_total[j] or 1.0
                for word in self.vocab:
                    self.emission_probs[j][word] = (
                        emit_counts[j].get(word, 0) / denom
                    )

            if verbose:
                print(f"  Iter {iteration + 1:2d}: log P(O|λ) = {total_ll:.4f}")

            # Early stopping: converged if improvement is tiny
            if abs(total_ll - prev_ll) < tol:
                if verbose:
                    print(f"  Converged at iteration {iteration + 1}.")
                break
            prev_ll = total_ll

        return log_likes

    def _backward(self, sentence: List[str]) -> List[Dict[str, float]]:
        """Compute backward variables β_t(j) for Baum-Welch.

        β_T(j) = 1  for all j
        β_t(j) = Σ_k  A[j][k] · B[k][o_{t+1}] · β_{t+1}(k)
        """
        tags = list(self.tags)
        T    = len(sentence)
        beta: List[Dict[str, float]] = [{}] * T

        # Initialization: β at last time step = 1
        beta[T - 1] = {j: 1.0 for j in tags}

        # Recursion: work backwards from T-2 to 0
        for t in range(T - 2, -1, -1):
            bt = {}
            for j in tags:
                bt[j] = sum(
                    self.transition_probs[j][k]
                    * self._emission(k, sentence[t + 1])
                    * beta[t + 1][k]
                    for k in tags
                )
            beta[t] = bt

        return beta

    # ── Evaluation ──────────────────────────────────────────────────

    def evaluate(self, test_sentences: List[List[Tuple[str, str]]],
                verbose: bool = False) -> float:
        """Token accuracy + optional per-tag confusion breakdown.

        Args:
            test_sentences: Tagged sentences to evaluate on.
            verbose:        Print per-tag precision/recall if True.

        Returns:
            Overall token accuracy (0.0–1.0).
        """
        correct = total = 0
        # confusion[gold][pred] = count of gold→pred mismatches
        confusion: Dict[str, Counter] = defaultdict(Counter)

        for sentence in test_sentences:
            words      = [w for w, _ in sentence]
            gold_tags  = [t for _, t in sentence]
            pred_tags  = self.viterbi(words)
            for gold, pred in zip(gold_tags, pred_tags):
                confusion[gold][pred] += 1
                if gold == pred:
                    correct += 1
                total += 1

        accuracy = correct / total if total > 0 else 0.0

        if verbose:
            print(f"\n  Overall accuracy: {accuracy:.2%}  ({correct}/{total})\n")
            print(f"  {'Tag':6s}  {'Correct':>8s}  {'Total':>6s}  {'Acc':>6s}  Most-confused-with")
            print(f"  {'─'*55}")
            for tag in sorted(confusion):
                row        = confusion[tag]
                tag_total  = sum(row.values())
                tag_ok     = row.get(tag, 0)
                tag_acc    = tag_ok / tag_total if tag_total > 0 else 0.0
                worst      = [(p, c) for p, c in row.items() if p != tag]
                worst_str  = (
                    f"→{worst[0][0]}({worst[0][1]})"
                    if worst else "—"
                )
                print(f"  {tag:6s}  {tag_ok:8d}  {tag_total:6d}  {tag_acc:5.1%}  {worst_str}")

        return accuracy

    # ── Utilities ───────────────────────────────────────────────────

    def _print_trellis(self, sentence: List[str], tags: List[str],
                       table: List[Dict[str, float]],
                       label: str = "Trellis") -> None:
        """Print a readable trellis table to stdout.

        Columns = time steps (words), rows = states (tags).
        Values are shown in scientific notation to handle small probabilities.
        """
        col_w = 12
        header = f"  {label}\n"
        header += f"  {'Tag':6s}"
        for word in sentence:
            header += f"  {word[:col_w]:>{col_w}}"
        print(header)
        print(f"  {'─' * (8 + (col_w + 2) * len(sentence))}")
        for tag in sorted(tags):
            row = f"  {tag:6s}"
            for t in range(len(sentence)):
                val = table[t].get(tag, 0)
                row += f"  {val:>{col_w}.3e}"
            print(row)
        print()
