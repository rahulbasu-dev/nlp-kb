Understanding CBOW

Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW) is a neural network architecture that learns word embeddings by predicting a target word from its surrounding context words - like a fill-in-the-blank game for machines.

Part of the Word2Vec Family

* Intuition

Understanding the core idea behind CBOW through analogies and everyday examples

What is CBOW?

CBOW stands for Continuous Bag of Words. It's a neural network model that learns to represent words as dense vectors (embeddings) by training on a simple task: predict the center word given its surrounding context words.

The key insight is elegantly simple: words that appear in similar contexts should have similar meanings, and therefore similar vector representations.

Fill-in-the-Blank Game

CBOW is like playing a fill-in-the-blank game:

  • Sentence: "The cat sat on the ___"
  • Context words: "The", "cat", "sat", "on", "the"
  • Target to predict: "mat" (or "floor", "couch", etc.)

By learning to predict the missing word from context, the model learns that "mat", "floor", and "couch" share something in common - they can all follow "sat on the"!

Context Window

"What words surround the target?"

The context window defines how many words to the left and right we look at when predicting the center word.

Example: Window Size = 2

For the sentence "The quick brown fox jumps":

The quick brown fox jumps over

Context: [quick, brown, jumps, over] -> Predict: "fox"

Why "Bag of Words"?

"Order doesn't matter!"

CBOW averages the context word vectors together, ignoring their order. It treats context as a "bag" - knowing which words are present, not their sequence.

Bag of Words Concept

These contexts are treated the same:

  • "quick brown ___ jumps over"
  • "brown quick ___ over jumps"

Both result in the same averaged context vector!

How CBOW Learns

The Classroom Analogy

Imagine learning vocabulary in a foreign language:

  • Instead of memorizing definitions, you see how words are used in sentences
  • If you see "delicious" near "food", "meal", "taste", you learn it relates to eating
  • If you see "fast" near "car", "run", "quick", you learn it relates to speed

CBOW learns word meanings the same way - purely from context, without any dictionaries!

The Core CBOW Idea

Context Words -> Average -> Predict Center Word

The training process:

  • Input: Context word embeddings (e.g., "quick", "brown", "jumps")
  • Average: Compute the mean of all context vectors
  • Predict: Use this average to predict the center word ("fox")
  • Update: Adjust embeddings to make better predictions

* Mathematical Foundations

The formal definitions and formulas behind CBOW

CBOW Objective Function

The goal of CBOW is to maximize the probability of predicting the correct center word given its context. This is formalized as:

CBOW Objective (Log-Likelihood)
\[ J(\theta) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(w_t | w_{t-c}, \ldots, w_{t-1}, w_{t+1}, \ldots, w_{t+c}) \]
Where:
  • \( T \) = Total number of words in the corpus
  • \( w_t \) = The target (center) word at position \( t \)
  • \( c \) = The context window size
  • \( w_{t-c}, \ldots, w_{t+c} \) = Context words within window

Context Vector Averaging

CBOW computes a hidden representation by averaging the input embeddings of all context words:

Hidden Layer (Average of Context Vectors)
\[ \mathbf{h} = \frac{1}{2c} \sum_{-c \leq j \leq c, j \neq 0} \mathbf{v}_{w_{t+j}} \]
Where:
  • \( \mathbf{h} \) = Hidden layer vector (averaged context)
  • \( \mathbf{v}_{w} \) = Input embedding vector for word \( w \)
  • \( 2c \) = Total number of context words (c on each side)

Why Averaging?

  • Simplicity: No learnable parameters in the combination
  • Symmetry: Order of context words doesn't affect result
  • Efficiency: Single vector regardless of context size
  • Normalization: Keeps values in reasonable range

Softmax Output Layer

The probability of each word being the target is computed using softmax over dot products:

Softmax Probability
\[ P(w_O | w_C) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{v}'_{w_O}{}^{\top} \mathbf{h})}{\sum_{w=1}^{V} \exp(\mathbf{v}'_w{}^{\top} \mathbf{h})} \]
Where:
  • \( \mathbf{v}'_{w_O} \) = Output embedding for target word \( w_O \)
  • \( \mathbf{h} \) = Hidden layer (averaged context)
  • \( V \) = Vocabulary size
  • Numerator: Score for correct word
  • Denominator: Sum of scores for ALL words (normalization)

Computational Challenge

The denominator sums over ALL words in the vocabulary (often 100K+ words). This makes training slow!

Solutions: Hierarchical Softmax or Negative Sampling (see comparison section).

Loss Function and Gradients

We minimize the negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy loss):

Loss Function
\[ \mathcal{L} = -\log P(w_O | w_C) = -\mathbf{v}'_{w_O}{}^{\top} \mathbf{h} + \log \sum_{w=1}^{V} \exp(\mathbf{v}'_w{}^{\top} \mathbf{h}) \]

Gradients for Backpropagation

Gradient w.r.t. Output Embedding
\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{v}'_w} = \left( P(w|w_C) - \mathbf{1}_{w=w_O} \right) \mathbf{h} \]

Where \( \mathbf{1}_{w=w_O} \) is 1 if \( w \) is the target word, 0 otherwise.

  • For the correct word: gradient pushes embedding closer to \( \mathbf{h} \)
  • For incorrect words: gradient pushes embeddings away from \( \mathbf{h} \)
Gradient w.r.t. Hidden Layer
\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{h}} = \sum_{w=1}^{V} \left( P(w|w_C) - \mathbf{1}_{w=w_O} \right) \mathbf{v}'_w \]
Gradient w.r.t. Input Embeddings
\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{v}_{w_i}} = \frac{1}{2c} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{h}} \quad \text{for each context word } w_i \]

Each context word's embedding is updated by the same amount (scaled by 1/2c due to averaging).

Two Sets of Embeddings

CBOW maintains two separate embedding matrices:

Matrix Symbol Shape Purpose
Input Embeddings \( \mathbf{W} \) or \( \mathbf{v} \) \( V \times d \) Represent context words
Output Embeddings \( \mathbf{W}' \) or \( \mathbf{v}' \) \( V \times d \) Compute prediction scores

Which Embeddings to Use?

After training, you have options:

  • Input embeddings only: Most common choice
  • Output embeddings only: Less common
  • Average both: Sometimes improves quality
  • Concatenate both: Doubles dimensionality

* CBOW Architecture Flowchart

Visual representation of the CBOW neural network architecture

Architecture Components Explained

1

Input Layer (Context Words)

Context words are converted to one-hot vectors of size V (vocabulary size). Each context word is represented as a sparse vector with a single 1.

2

Projection Layer (Embedding Lookup)

One-hot vectors are multiplied by the embedding matrix W (V x d) to get dense d-dimensional vectors. This is equivalent to looking up the row corresponding to the word.

3

Hidden Layer (Averaging)

All context word embeddings are averaged element-wise to produce a single d-dimensional hidden vector h. This is the "bag of words" step - order is lost.

4

Output Layer (Softmax)

The hidden vector h is multiplied by output embedding matrix W' (V x d) to get scores for each word. Softmax converts these to probabilities summing to 1.

* Python Implementation

Complete, runnable code implementing CBOW from scratch

Complete CBOW Implementation

Copy this code into a Python file and run it directly. Only uses NumPy!

"""
CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words) Implementation from Scratch
==========================================================
Educational implementation - clear, commented, runnable.
"""

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
import re

class CBOW:
    """Continuous Bag of Words model for learning word embeddings."""

    def __init__(self, embedding_dim=50, window_size=2, learning_rate=0.01):
        """
        Initialize CBOW model.

        Args:
            embedding_dim: Dimension of word embeddings
            window_size: Number of context words on each side
            learning_rate: Learning rate for gradient descent
        """
        self.embedding_dim = embedding_dim
        self.window_size = window_size
        self.learning_rate = learning_rate
        self.word_to_idx = {}
        self.idx_to_word = {}
        self.W = None   # Input embeddings (V x d)
        self.W_prime = None  # Output embeddings (V x d)

    def preprocess(self, text):
        """Tokenize text into words."""
        return re.findall(r'[a-z]+', text.lower())

    def build_vocabulary(self, corpus):
        """Build word-to-index mapping from corpus."""
        word_counts = Counter()
        for sentence in corpus:
            tokens = self.preprocess(sentence)
            word_counts.update(tokens)

        # Create vocabulary (sorted for reproducibility)
        vocabulary = sorted(word_counts.keys())
        self.word_to_idx = {word: i for i, word in enumerate(vocabulary)}
        self.idx_to_word = {i: word for word, i in self.word_to_idx.items()}

        return len(vocabulary)

    def initialize_embeddings(self, vocab_size):
        """Initialize embedding matrices with small random values."""
        self.W = np.random.randn(vocab_size, self.embedding_dim) * 0.01
        self.W_prime = np.random.randn(vocab_size, self.embedding_dim) * 0.01

    def generate_training_data(self, corpus):
        """Generate (context_indices, target_index) pairs."""
        training_data = []

        for sentence in corpus:
            tokens = self.preprocess(sentence)
            indices = [self.word_to_idx[w] for w in tokens if w in self.word_to_idx]

            for i, target_idx in enumerate(indices):
                # Get context word indices
                start = max(0, i - self.window_size)
                end = min(len(indices), i + self.window_size + 1)

                context_indices = [indices[j] for j in range(start, end) if j != i]

                if context_indices:  # Only if we have context
                    training_data.append((context_indices, target_idx))

        return training_data

    def softmax(self, x):
        """Compute softmax with numerical stability."""
        exp_x = np.exp(x - np.max(x))
        return exp_x / exp_x.sum()

    def forward(self, context_indices):
        """
        Forward pass: context words -> hidden -> output probabilities.

        Returns:
            h: Hidden layer (averaged context embeddings)
            probs: Softmax probabilities over vocabulary
        """
        # Average context word embeddings
        context_embeddings = self.W[context_indices]
        h = np.mean(context_embeddings, axis=0)

        # Compute scores and softmax
        scores = np.dot(self.W_prime, h)
        probs = self.softmax(scores)

        return h, probs

    def backward(self, context_indices, target_idx, h, probs):
        """
        Backward pass: compute gradients and update embeddings.
        """
        # Gradient of loss w.r.t. scores (softmax output - one-hot target)
        grad_scores = probs.copy()
        grad_scores[target_idx] -= 1  # (p - y)

        # Gradient w.r.t. output embeddings W'
        # dL/dW' = grad_scores (outer) h
        grad_W_prime = np.outer(grad_scores, h)

        # Gradient w.r.t. hidden layer h
        grad_h = np.dot(self.W_prime.T, grad_scores)

        # Gradient w.r.t. input embeddings W (for context words only)
        # Each context word gets grad_h / num_context_words
        grad_context = grad_h / len(context_indices)

        # Update output embeddings
        self.W_prime -= self.learning_rate * grad_W_prime

        # Update input embeddings for context words
        for idx in context_indices:
            self.W[idx] -= self.learning_rate * grad_context

    def compute_loss(self, probs, target_idx):
        """Compute cross-entropy loss."""
        return -np.log(probs[target_idx] + 1e-10)

    def train(self, corpus, epochs=100, verbose=True):
        """
        Train the CBOW model on a corpus.

        Args:
            corpus: List of sentences
            epochs: Number of training epochs
            verbose: Print progress
        """
        # Build vocabulary and initialize
        vocab_size = self.build_vocabulary(corpus)
        self.initialize_embeddings(vocab_size)

        # Generate training data
        training_data = self.generate_training_data(corpus)

        print(f"Vocabulary size: {vocab_size}")
        print(f"Training examples: {len(training_data)}")

        # Training loop
        for epoch in range(epochs):
            total_loss = 0
            np.random.shuffle(training_data)

            for context_indices, target_idx in training_data:
                # Forward pass
                h, probs = self.forward(context_indices)

                # Compute loss
                loss = self.compute_loss(probs, target_idx)
                total_loss += loss

                # Backward pass
                self.backward(context_indices, target_idx, h, probs)

            if verbose and (epoch + 1) % 10 == 0:
                avg_loss = total_loss / len(training_data)
                print(f"Epoch {epoch+1}/{epochs}, Loss: {avg_loss:.4f}")

    def get_embedding(self, word):
        """Get the embedding vector for a word."""
        if word not in self.word_to_idx:
            return None
        return self.W[self.word_to_idx[word]]

    def most_similar(self, word, top_n=5):
        """Find most similar words by cosine similarity."""
        if word not in self.word_to_idx:
            return []

        word_vec = self.get_embedding(word)
        similarities = []

        for other_word, idx in self.word_to_idx.items():
            if other_word == word:
                continue
            other_vec = self.W[idx]
            # Cosine similarity
            sim = np.dot(word_vec, other_vec) / (
                np.linalg.norm(word_vec) * np.linalg.norm(other_vec) + 1e-10
            )
            similarities.append((other_word, sim))

        return sorted(similarities, key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)[:top_n]

    def predict(self, context_words):
        """Predict the most likely center word given context."""
        context_indices = [self.word_to_idx[w] for w in context_words
                          if w in self.word_to_idx]
        if not context_indices:
            return None

        h, probs = self.forward(context_indices)
        top_idx = np.argmax(probs)
        return self.idx_to_word[top_idx], probs[top_idx]


# Example usage
if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Sample corpus
    corpus = [
        "the king loves the queen",
        "the queen loves the king",
        "the king and queen rule the kingdom",
        "the man loves the woman",
        "the woman loves the man",
        "the prince will become king",
        "the princess will become queen",
        "royalty rules the kingdom",
        "the cat sat on the mat",
        "the dog sat on the rug",
        "cats and dogs are pets",
        "pets are animals",
    ]

    # Create and train model
    model = CBOW(embedding_dim=20, window_size=2, learning_rate=0.05)
    model.train(corpus, epochs=200)

    # Find similar words
    print("\nMost similar to 'king':")
    for word, sim in model.most_similar('king'):
        print(f"  {word}: {sim:.4f}")

    print("\nMost similar to 'cat':")
    for word, sim in model.most_similar('cat'):
        print(f"  {word}: {sim:.4f}")

    # Predict center word from context
    print("\nPredicting center word:")
    context = ['the', 'loves', 'the', 'queen']
    prediction, prob = model.predict(context)
    print(f"  Context: {context}")
    print(f"  Predicted: '{prediction}' (probability: {prob:.4f})")

* CBOW vs Skip-gram Comparison

Understanding when to use CBOW versus Skip-gram

Architecture Comparison

Aspect CBOW Skip-gram
Input Multiple context words Single center word
Output Single center word Multiple context words
Prediction Task P(center | context) P(context | center)
Training Speed Faster (one prediction per window) Slower (multiple predictions per window)
Frequent Words Better representations May underperform
Rare Words May underperform Better representations
Memory Usage Lower (fewer gradients) Higher (more gradients)

When to Use CBOW

CBOW is Better For:

  • Large datasets - Faster training
  • Frequent words - Better at common words
  • Syntactic tasks - Captures grammar patterns
  • Limited compute - Less memory intensive
  • Real-time applications - Quicker updates

Use Case Examples

  • Autocomplete suggestions
  • Spell checking
  • Text preprocessing
  • Quick prototyping

When to Use Skip-gram

Skip-gram is Better For:

  • Small datasets - More training signal
  • Rare words - Better at uncommon words
  • Semantic tasks - Captures meaning
  • Analogy tasks - king - man + woman = queen
  • Quality over speed - When time allows

Use Case Examples

  • Semantic search
  • Word analogies
  • Clustering words by meaning
  • Transfer learning base

Training Efficiency Comparison

For a single center word with window size c=2 (4 context words):

CBOW

1 forward pass: Average 4 context -> predict 1 center

1 backward pass: Update all embeddings once

Total: 1 training example per window position

Skip-gram

4 forward passes: Center -> predict each context word

4 backward passes: Update embeddings 4 times

Total: 4 training examples per window position

Why Skip-gram is Slower but Sometimes Better

Skip-gram creates more training examples from the same data. For rare words, this extra signal helps learn better representations. CBOW averages context, which smooths out rare word contributions.

The "Bag of Words" Trade-off

Understanding the Trade-off

Consider these two contexts for predicting "bank":

  • Context 1: "walked to the ___ yesterday"
  • Context 2: "river near the ___ flooded"

CBOW averages context words, potentially mixing signals. Skip-gram treats each context word individually, preserving more specific associations.

This is why Skip-gram often produces better quality embeddings, while CBOW trains faster.

Practical Recommendations

Scenario Recommendation Reason
Billions of words, fast training needed CBOW 4x faster training
Small corpus, quality matters Skip-gram Better rare word handling
Domain with many rare terms Skip-gram More training signal per word
General-purpose embeddings Skip-gram + NS Best overall quality
Online/streaming training CBOW Lower memory, faster updates
