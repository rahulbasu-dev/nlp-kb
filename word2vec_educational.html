Understanding Word2Vec

Word2Vec transforms words into dense vector representations that capture semantic meaning, allowing machines to understand that "king" and "queen" are related, and enabling remarkable word analogies like king - man + woman = queen.[1]

Neural Approach to Word Embeddings

* Intuition

Understanding the core idea behind Word2Vec through analogies and real-world examples

What is Word2Vec?

Word2Vec is a family of neural network models that learn to represent words as dense vectors (lists of numbers) in a continuous vector space.[1] Unlike sparse representations like TF-IDF where most values are zero, Word2Vec creates compact vectors where every dimension carries meaning.

The fundamental insight is captured by the distributional hypothesis:

The Distributional Hypothesis

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps." - J.R. Firth (1957)

Words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. Word2Vec learns these patterns from massive amounts of text, discovering that "dog" and "cat" often appear near similar words (pet, food, vet), so they must be related.[1][2]

The Map Analogy

Imagine creating a map where every word is a city. Word2Vec arranges these cities so that:

  • Similar words are close together: "happy", "joyful", and "cheerful" are neighboring towns.
  • Relationships are preserved: The distance from "Paris" to "France" is similar to "Tokyo" to "Japan".
  • Directions have meaning: Moving in the "capital-of" direction from any country lands you near its capital.

This "semantic map" lets computers reason about word relationships mathematically!

Skip-gram Model

"Given a word, predict its context."[1]

Skip-gram takes a center word and tries to predict the words that surround it. This forces the model to learn which words tend to appear together.

Example

Given the sentence: "The quick brown fox jumps over"

If "fox" is the center word with window size 2:

  • Input: "fox"
  • Predict: "quick", "brown", "jumps", "over"

Best for: Rare words, smaller datasets

CBOW Model

"Given the context, predict the word."[1]

Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW) takes the surrounding words and tries to predict the center word. It's the reverse of Skip-gram.

Example

Given the sentence: "The quick brown ___ jumps over"

With window size 2:

  • Input: "quick", "brown", "jumps", "over"
  • Predict: "fox"

Best for: Frequent words, larger datasets, faster training

* Worked Examples

Concrete examples of word relationships, analogies, and similarity scores

The Famous Analogy: King - Man + Woman = Queen

The most celebrated property of Word2Vec is that vector arithmetic captures semantic relationships:[2]

king - man + woman = queen

How Does This Work?

The vectors encode multiple semantic dimensions. By subtracting "man" from "king", we remove the "maleness" component, leaving "royalty". Adding "woman" then gives us "female royalty" = "queen"!

In vector notation: vec("king") - vec("man") + vec("woman") ≈ vec("queen")

Limitations

Word2Vec analogies are not perfect. They work best for common, unambiguous relationships.[5] The model can also encode biases present in the training data (e.g., gender stereotypes in professions).

Word Similarity Scores

Word2Vec measures similarity using cosine similarity - the cosine of the angle between two vectors. A score of 1 means identical, 0 means unrelated, -1 means opposite.

Why Are Antonyms Similar?

"Good" and "bad" have high similarity because they appear in similar contexts: "The movie was good/bad", "a good/bad idea". Word2Vec captures relatedness, not just synonymy. This is actually useful - it groups semantically related concepts together.

* Mathematical Foundations

The neural network architecture and loss functions behind Word2Vec

Skip-gram Model Architecture

Skip-gram is a shallow neural network with one hidden layer. Given a center word, it predicts the probability of each context word.[1][4]

Skip-gram Objective
\[ \text{maximize} \quad \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{-c \le j \le c, j \ne 0} \log P(w_{t+j} | w_t) \]

Where:

  • \( T \) = Total number of words in the corpus
  • \( c \) = Context window size (typically 5-10)
  • \( w_t \) = Center word at position \( t \)
  • \( w_{t+j} \) = Context word at offset \( j \) from center

The probability is computed using the softmax function over all words in the vocabulary:

Softmax Probability
\[ P(w_O | w_I) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{v}'_{w_O} \cdot \mathbf{v}_{w_I})}{\sum_{w=1}^{V} \exp(\mathbf{v}'_w \cdot \mathbf{v}_{w_I})} \]
  • \( \mathbf{v}_{w_I} \) = Input (center word) embedding vector
  • \( \mathbf{v}'_{w_O} \) = Output (context word) embedding vector
  • \( V \) = Vocabulary size

Note: Word2Vec maintains two sets of vectors - input and output embeddings. Usually, only the input embeddings are used after training.

The Softmax Problem and Solutions

Computing the full softmax requires summing over the entire vocabulary (often 100,000+ words) for every training example. This is computationally prohibitive![2][3]

Computational Cost

For vocabulary size V = 100,000 and embedding dimension d = 300:

Each softmax computation requires 100,000 dot products and exponentiations - billions of operations per training epoch!

Solution 1: Hierarchical Softmax

Organize vocabulary in a binary tree. Instead of computing over all words, traverse the tree from root to target word, making binary decisions at each node.

Hierarchical Softmax Complexity
\[ O(V) \rightarrow O(\log_2 V) \]

Reduces computation from linear to logarithmic in vocabulary size. For V = 100,000, this is a 6,000x speedup!

Solution 2: Negative Sampling (Most Popular)

Instead of computing probabilities for all words, only update the correct word and a small sample of "negative" (incorrect) words.[2][3]

Negative Sampling Objective
\[ \log \sigma(\mathbf{v}'_{w_O} \cdot \mathbf{v}_{w_I}) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbb{E}_{w_i \sim P_n(w)} \left[ \log \sigma(-\mathbf{v}'_{w_i} \cdot \mathbf{v}_{w_I}) \right] \]
  • \( \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \) is the sigmoid function
  • \( k \) = Number of negative samples (typically 5-20)
  • \( P_n(w) \) = Noise distribution (usually \( U(w)^{3/4} / Z \) )

The model learns to distinguish real context pairs from random noise pairs.[3]

CBOW Model Architecture

CBOW reverses Skip-gram: it predicts the center word from the surrounding context words.[1][4]

CBOW Objective
\[ \text{maximize} \quad \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(w_t | w_{t-c}, \ldots, w_{t-1}, w_{t+1}, \ldots, w_{t+c}) \]

The context representation is typically the average of all context word vectors:

\[ \mathbf{h} = \frac{1}{2c} \sum_{-c \le j \le c, j \ne 0} \mathbf{v}_{w_{t+j}} \]
Property Skip-gram CBOW
InputCenter wordContext words (averaged)
OutputContext wordsCenter word
Training SpeedSlowerFaster
Rare WordsBetterWorse
Training PairsMore (one per context word)Fewer (one per window)

Cosine Similarity

Word similarity is measured using cosine similarity - the cosine of the angle between two vectors:

Cosine Similarity
\[ \text{cos}(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{||\mathbf{A}|| \cdot ||\mathbf{B}||} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i B_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i^2} \cdot \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} B_i^2}} \]
  • Result ranges from -1 (opposite) to 1 (identical)
  • 0 indicates orthogonal/unrelated vectors
  • Independent of vector magnitude (focuses on direction)

* Word2Vec Training Pipeline

Visual representation of the complete Word2Vec training process

Process Steps Explained

1

Preprocessing

Tokenization: Split text into words. Vocabulary: Build word-to-index mapping. Subsampling: Randomly remove frequent words (like "the") to balance training.

2

Generate Training Pairs

Slide a window across the text. For each position, create (center, context) pairs. Window size c typically ranges from 2-10 words on each side.

3

Neural Network Forward Pass

Skip-gram: Look up center word embedding, predict context probabilities. CBOW: Average context embeddings, predict center word probability.

4

Loss and Backpropagation

Compute loss using negative sampling (most common) or hierarchical softmax. Update embeddings via gradient descent to make correct predictions more likely.

Using Gensim (Recommended for Production)

Gensim provides an optimized, battle-tested implementation of Word2Vec.

"""
Word2Vec with Gensim
====================
Production-ready implementation with pre-trained models.
"""

from gensim.models import Word2Vec
from gensim.models import KeyedVectors

# Sample corpus (list of tokenized sentences)
corpus = [
    ["the", "king", "loves", "the", "queen"],
    ["the", "queen", "rules", "the", "kingdom"],
    ["a", "man", "works", "in", "the", "kingdom"],
    ["a", "woman", "lives", "in", "the", "castle"],
    ["the", "prince", "is", "young"],
    ["the", "princess", "is", "beautiful"],
]

# Train Word2Vec model
model = Word2Vec(
    sentences=corpus,
    vector_size=100,    # Embedding dimension
    window=5,            # Context window size
    min_count=1,         # Minimum word frequency
    sg=1,                # 1 = Skip-gram, 0 = CBOW
    negative=5,          # Negative samples
    epochs=100           # Training iterations
)

# Get word vector
vector = model.wv["king"]
print(f"Vector for 'king': {vector[:5]}...")

# Find similar words
similar = model.wv.most_similar("king", topn=3)
print(f"Similar to 'king': {similar}")

# Word analogy: king - man + woman = ?
result = model.wv.most_similar(
    positive=["king", "woman"],
    negative=["man"],
    topn=1
)
print(f"king - man + woman = {result}")

# Compute similarity between two words
sim = model.wv.similarity("king", "queen")
print(f"Similarity(king, queen): {sim:.4f}")

# Save and load model
model.save("word2vec.model")
loaded_model = Word2Vec.load("word2vec.model")

Word2Vec from Scratch (Educational)

A simplified Skip-gram implementation with negative sampling to understand the core concepts.

"""
Word2Vec Skip-gram from Scratch
================================
Educational implementation showing core concepts.
"""

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
import random

class Word2VecSkipGram:
    """Simplified Skip-gram with Negative Sampling."""

    def __init__(self, embedding_dim=50, window_size=2,
                 neg_samples=5, learning_rate=0.01):
        self.embedding_dim = embedding_dim
        self.window_size = window_size
        self.neg_samples = neg_samples
        self.lr = learning_rate

    def build_vocab(self, sentences):
        """Build vocabulary from sentences."""
        word_counts = Counter(w for s in sentences for w in s)
        self.vocab = sorted(word_counts.keys())
        self.word2idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(self.vocab)}
        self.idx2word = {i: w for w, i in self.word2idx.items()}
        self.vocab_size = len(self.vocab)

        # Negative sampling distribution: P(w)^0.75
        counts = np.array([word_counts[w] for w in self.vocab])
        self.neg_dist = np.power(counts, 0.75)
        self.neg_dist /= self.neg_dist.sum()

    def init_embeddings(self):
        """Initialize embedding matrices."""
        # Input embeddings (center words)
        self.W_in = np.random.randn(self.vocab_size, self.embedding_dim) * 0.01
        # Output embeddings (context words)
        self.W_out = np.random.randn(self.vocab_size, self.embedding_dim) * 0.01

    def generate_training_pairs(self, sentences):
        """Generate (center, context) training pairs."""
        pairs = []
        for sentence in sentences:
            indices = [self.word2idx[w] for w in sentence if w in self.word2idx]
            for i, center in enumerate(indices):
                for j in range(max(0, i-self.window_size),
                               min(len(indices), i+self.window_size+1)):
                    if i != j:
                        pairs.append((center, indices[j]))
        return pairs

    def sigmoid(self, x):
        """Numerically stable sigmoid."""
        return np.where(x >= 0,
                        1 / (1 + np.exp(-x)),
                        np.exp(x) / (1 + np.exp(x)))

    def train_pair(self, center_idx, context_idx):
        """Train on one (center, context) pair with negative sampling."""
        # Get center word embedding
        h = self.W_in[center_idx]

        # Positive sample: context word (label = 1)
        v_pos = self.W_out[context_idx]
        score_pos = self.sigmoid(np.dot(h, v_pos))
        grad_pos = (score_pos - 1) * h

        # Negative samples: random words (label = 0)
        neg_indices = np.random.choice(self.vocab_size, self.neg_samples,
                                       p=self.neg_dist)
        grad_h = (score_pos - 1) * v_pos

        for neg_idx in neg_indices:
            if neg_idx == context_idx:
                continue
            v_neg = self.W_out[neg_idx]
            score_neg = self.sigmoid(np.dot(h, v_neg))
            grad_neg = score_neg * h
            grad_h += score_neg * v_neg
            # Update negative output embedding
            self.W_out[neg_idx] -= self.lr * grad_neg

        # Update positive output embedding
        self.W_out[context_idx] -= self.lr * grad_pos
        # Update input embedding
        self.W_in[center_idx] -= self.lr * grad_h

    def train(self, sentences, epochs=10):
        """Train the model."""
        self.build_vocab(sentences)
        self.init_embeddings()
        pairs = self.generate_training_pairs(sentences)

        for epoch in range(epochs):
            random.shuffle(pairs)
            for center, context in pairs:
                self.train_pair(center, context)
            print(f"Epoch {epoch+1}/{epochs} completed")

    def get_embedding(self, word):
        """Get embedding for a word."""
        if word not in self.word2idx:
            return None
        return self.W_in[self.word2idx[word]]

    def similarity(self, word1, word2):
        """Compute cosine similarity between two words."""
        v1, v2 = self.get_embedding(word1), self.get_embedding(word2)
        if v1 is None or v2 is None:
            return None
        return np.dot(v1, v2) / (np.linalg.norm(v1) * np.linalg.norm(v2))

    def most_similar(self, word, topn=5):
        """Find most similar words."""
        if word not in self.word2idx:
            return []
        v = self.get_embedding(word)
        similarities = []
        for w in self.vocab:
            if w != word:
                sim = self.similarity(word, w)
                if sim is not None:
                    similarities.append((w, sim))
        return sorted(similarities, key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)[:topn]

# Example usage
if __name__ == "__main__":
    sentences = [
        ["the", "king", "loves", "the", "queen"],
        ["the", "queen", "rules", "the", "kingdom"],
        ["a", "man", "works", "in", "the", "kingdom"],
        ["a", "woman", "lives", "in", "the", "castle"],
        ["the", "prince", "is", "young"],
        ["the", "princess", "is", "beautiful"],
    ]

    model = Word2VecSkipGram(embedding_dim=50, window_size=2)
    model.train(sentences, epochs=100)

    print("\nSimilar to 'king':")
    for word, score in model.most_similar("king", 3):
        print(f"  {word}: {score:.4f}")

Applications of Word2Vec

Semantic Search

Find documents containing synonyms or related terms, not just exact keyword matches. Search for "automobile" and find documents about "car".

Recommendation Systems

Treat products as "words" and user sessions as "sentences" to learn item embeddings. Similar items have similar vectors.

Named Entity Recognition

Use word embeddings as features for sequence labeling models. Words with similar syntactic roles cluster together.

Machine Translation

Embed words from different languages into the same space. "roi" (French) and "king" (English) become neighbors.

When to Use Word2Vec vs. Modern Alternatives

  • Word2Vec: Fast training, interpretable, works well for many tasks. Good baseline.[1][2]
  • FastText: Handles out-of-vocabulary words via subword embeddings. Better for morphologically rich languages.
  • GloVe: Combines count-based and neural approaches. Often similar performance to Word2Vec.
  • BERT/GPT: Contextual embeddings (same word, different vectors by context). State-of-the-art but much heavier.

Word2Vec has been shown to implicitly perform matrix factorization on a word-context co-occurrence matrix.[5]

* Simple Numerical Examples

Hands-on calculations with small vectors to understand the math behind Word2Vec

The King-Queen Analogy with Real Numbers

Let's work through the famous analogy using simplified 3-dimensional vectors. In practice, Word2Vec uses 100-300 dimensions, but the same principles apply.[2]

Simplified Word Vectors (3D)

Imagine our 3 dimensions encode: [royalty, gender (male=1), age]

king =
1 1 1
(royal, male, adult)
man =
0 1 1
(not royal, male, adult)
woman =
0 0 1
(not royal, female, adult)
queen =
1 0 1
(royal, female, adult)

Step-by-Step Vector Arithmetic

King - Man + Woman = ?

Step 1: king - man

[1, 1, 1] - [0, 1, 1] = [1, 0, 0]

(This extracts the "royalty" concept)


Step 2: (king - man) + woman

[1, 0, 0] + [0, 0, 1] = [1, 0, 1]

(Add "femaleness" to royalty)


Result: [1, 0, 1] = queen!

The vector [1, 0, 1] exactly matches our "queen" vector. In real Word2Vec, the result would be close to (not exactly) the queen vector, and we would use cosine similarity to find the nearest word.

Cosine Similarity Calculation

Let's calculate the cosine similarity between "king" and "queen" step-by-step.

Cosine Similarity Formula
\[ \text{cos}(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{||\mathbf{A}|| \cdot ||\mathbf{B}||} \]

Example: Similarity between king [1, 1, 1] and queen [1, 0, 1]

Step 1: Calculate the dot product (A . B)

A . B = (1)(1) + (1)(0) + (1)(1) = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2


Step 2: Calculate the magnitude of A (||A||)

||A|| = sqrt(1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2) = sqrt(3) = 1.732


Step 3: Calculate the magnitude of B (||B||)

||B|| = sqrt(1^2 + 0^2 + 1^2) = sqrt(2) = 1.414


Step 4: Compute cosine similarity

cos(theta) = 2 / (1.732 x 1.414) = 2 / 2.449 = 0.816


Interpretation: A similarity of 0.816 indicates "king" and "queen" are highly similar, which makes sense - they share the "royalty" and "adult" dimensions!
