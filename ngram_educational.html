Understanding N-gram Language Models

N-gram models predict the next word based on the previous N-1 words, powering everything from autocomplete to text generation before the neural era.

Probabilistic Sequence Modeling

💡 Intuition

Understanding N-grams through everyday examples

What is an N-gram Language Model?

An N-gram language model predicts the next word in a sequence based on the previous N-1 words. It's a statistical approach that learns patterns from text data.

📱 The Autocomplete Analogy

When you type on your phone keyboard and it suggests the next word, that's an N-gram model at work! After you type "I am", it might suggest "going" or "happy" because those sequences appeared frequently in its training data.

If millions of people typed "I am going", the model learns that P(going | I am) is high.

The Markov Assumption

N-gram models make a simplifying assumption: the next word depends only on the last N-1 words, not the entire history.

Markov Assumption (Bigram Example)
\[ P(w_k | w_1 w_2 \ldots w_{k-1}) \approx P(w_k | w_{k-1}) \]

Instead of considering the entire history \(w_1 \ldots w_{k-1}\), we only look at the immediately preceding word \(w_{k-1}\).

Types of N-grams

Unigram (N=1)

Each word is independent. \( P(w_k) \) doesn't depend on previous words.

Example: \( P(\text{cat}) = \text{count}(\text{cat}) / \text{total words} \)

Bigram (N=2)

Next word depends on the previous 1 word.

Example: \( P(\text{meow} | \text{cat}) \)

Trigram (N=3)

Next word depends on the previous 2 words.

Example: \( P(\text{loudly} | \text{cat meow}) \)

Concrete Examples

📝 Sentence Breakdown

Sentence: "The cat sat on the mat"

Bigrams:

  • <s> The
  • The cat
  • cat sat
  • sat on
  • on the
  • the mat
  • mat </s>

Trigrams:

  • <s> <s> The
  • <s> The cat
  • The cat sat
  • cat sat on

⚠️ Limitation: Long-Range Dependencies

N-gram models cannot capture long-range dependencies. Consider:

"The cat that the dog chased ___"

The correct answer is "ran" (agreeing with "cat"), but a bigram model only sees "chased ___" and might predict "the" or "away". The subject-verb agreement spans too many words.

🔢 N-gram Mathematics

The formal probability equations behind N-grams

Foundation: The Chain Rule

The probability of a sentence is the product of conditional probabilities:

Chain Rule of Probability
\[ P(w_1 w_2 \ldots w_n) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} P(w_k | w_1 \ldots w_{k-1}) \]

Each word's probability depends on all words that came before it. This is exact but computationally intractable for long sequences.

The N-gram Approximation

Bigram Approximation (N=2)
\[ P(w_k | w_1 \ldots w_{k-1}) \approx P(w_k | w_{k-1}) \]

We assume the next word depends only on the immediately previous word.

General N-gram Approximation
\[ P(w_k | w_1 \ldots w_{k-1}) \approx P(w_k | w_{k-N+1} \ldots w_{k-1}) \]

The next word \(w_k\) depends only on the previous \(N-1\) words.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

We estimate N-gram probabilities by counting occurrences in a corpus:

Bigram MLE
\[ P(w_n | w_{n-1}) = \frac{C(w_{n-1} w_n)}{C(w_{n-1})} \]

Where \(C(w_{n-1} w_n)\) = count of bigram "\(w_{n-1} w_n\)"
and \(C(w_{n-1})\) = count of unigram "\(w_{n-1}\)"

General N-gram MLE
\[ P(w_n | w_{n-N+1} \ldots w_{n-1}) = \frac{C(w_{n-N+1} \ldots w_n)}{C(w_{n-N+1} \ldots w_{n-1})} \]

Count how often the full N-gram appears, divided by how often the context (first N-1 words) appears.

Sentence Boundaries

We add special tokens to handle sentence starts and ends:

Sentence Boundary Tokens

\( \langle s \rangle \) = sentence start marker
\( \langle /s \rangle \) = sentence end marker

For a bigram model, we pad the sentence: <s> The cat sat </s>

This allows us to compute \( P(\text{The} | \langle s \rangle) \) and \( P(\langle /s \rangle | \text{sat}) \)

Step-by-step calculations with a real corpus

Example Corpus

📚 Training Data

Corpus: "I am Sam. Sam I am. I do not like green eggs and ham."

Sentences with boundaries:

  • <s> I am Sam </s>
  • <s> Sam I am </s>
  • <s> I do not like green eggs and ham </s>

Step 1: Count Bigrams

Bigram Count Context Count Probability
<s> I2C(<s>) = 32/3 = 0.667
<s> Sam1C(<s>) = 31/3 = 0.333
I am2C(I) = 32/3 = 0.667
I do1C(I) = 31/3 = 0.333
am Sam1C(am) = 21/2 = 0.500
am </s>1C(am) = 21/2 = 0.500
Sam I1C(Sam) = 21/2 = 0.500
Sam </s>1C(Sam) = 21/2 = 0.500

Step 2: Calculate Sentence Probability

Let's compute \( P(\text{<s> I am Sam </s>}) \) using our bigram model:

Sentence Probability Calculation
\[ \begin{align} P(\text{I am Sam}) &= P(\text{I} | \langle s \rangle) \times P(\text{am} | \text{I}) \\ &\quad \times P(\text{Sam} | \text{am}) \times P(\langle /s \rangle | \text{Sam}) \\ &= \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} \\ &= 0.667 \times 0.667 \times 0.500 \times 0.500 \\ &= \mathbf{0.111} \end{align} \]

Step 3: The Zero Problem

⚠️ The Zero Probability Problem

What if we try to compute \( P(\text{Sam likes eggs}) \)?

\( P(\text{likes} | \text{Sam}) = \frac{C(\text{Sam likes})}{C(\text{Sam})} = \frac{0}{2} = 0 \)

Problem: The entire sentence probability becomes zero! This is too harsh—just because we never saw "Sam likes" doesn't mean it's impossible.

Solution: Smoothing techniques (next section)

✏️ Try It Yourself!

Using the bigram counts above, calculate:

\( P(\text{<s> Sam I am </s>}) \)

Solution:

\( P(\text{Sam I am}) = P(\text{Sam} | \langle s \rangle) \times P(\text{I} | \text{Sam}) \times P(\text{am} | \text{I}) \times P(\langle /s \rangle | \text{am}) \)

\( = \frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{1}{2} \)

\( = 0.333 \times 0.500 \times 0.667 \times 0.500 \)

\( = \mathbf{0.0556} \)

🎨 Smoothing Techniques

Handling unseen N-grams to avoid zero probabilities

Why Smoothing?

In any finite corpus, most possible N-grams will have zero counts. Smoothing redistributes probability mass from seen to unseen events.

1. Laplace Smoothing (Add-1)

Add 1 to every count (pretend we saw every N-gram once more):

Laplace (Add-1) Smoothing
\[ P_{\text{Laplace}}(w_n | w_{n-1}) = \frac{C(w_{n-1} w_n) + 1}{C(w_{n-1}) + V} \]

Where \(V\) = vocabulary size (number of unique words)

Example with Our Corpus

Vocabulary: {I, am, Sam, do, not, like, green, eggs, and, ham, <s>, </s>} → V = 12

📊 Laplace Example

Before smoothing: \( P(\text{likes} | \text{Sam}) = 0/2 = 0 \)

After Laplace: \( P(\text{likes} | \text{Sam}) = \frac{0 + 1}{2 + 12} = \frac{1}{14} \approx 0.071 \)

Original bigram: \( P(\text{I} | \text{Sam}) = 1/2 = 0.500 \)

After Laplace: \( P(\text{I} | \text{Sam}) = \frac{1 + 1}{2 + 12} = \frac{2}{14} \approx 0.143 \)

Problem: Laplace drastically reduces probabilities of seen events!

2. Add-k Smoothing

A more flexible version: add a small constant k < 1:

Add-k Smoothing
\[ P_{\text{Add-k}}(w_n | w_{n-1}) = \frac{C(w_{n-1} w_n) + k}{C(w_{n-1}) + kV} \]

Typically k = 0.01 to 0.5. Tune k on a validation set.

📊 Add-k Example (k=0.1)

\( P(\text{likes} | \text{Sam}) = \frac{0 + 0.1}{2 + 0.1 \times 12} = \frac{0.1}{3.2} \approx 0.031 \)

\( P(\text{I} | \text{Sam}) = \frac{1 + 0.1}{2 + 1.2} = \frac{1.1}{3.2} \approx 0.344 \)

Better! Less distortion than Add-1.

3. Interpolation

Combine multiple N-gram orders, weighting each:

Linear Interpolation
\[ \hat{P}(w_n | w_{n-2} w_{n-1}) = \lambda_1 P(w_n | w_{n-2} w_{n-1}) + \lambda_2 P(w_n | w_{n-1}) + \lambda_3 P(w_n) \]

Where \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = 1\) and all \(\lambda_i \geq 0\)

Trigram + Bigram + Unigram. If trigram is unseen, bigram/unigram provide fallback.

📊 Interpolation Example

Suppose \(\lambda_1 = 0.6, \lambda_2 = 0.3, \lambda_3 = 0.1\)

If trigram = 0.5, bigram = 0.2, unigram = 0.01:

\( \hat{P} = 0.6(0.5) + 0.3(0.2) + 0.1(0.01) = 0.3 + 0.06 + 0.001 = 0.361 \)

✏️ Try It Yourself!

If the trigram "I like cats" has never been seen (P=0), but the bigram "like cats" has P=0.15 and unigram "cats" has P=0.02, what is the interpolated probability with λ₁=0.5, λ₂=0.3, λ₃=0.2?

Solution:

\( \hat{P} = 0.5(0) + 0.3(0.15) + 0.2(0.02) \)

\( = 0 + 0.045 + 0.004 = \mathbf{0.049} \)

Even though the trigram is unseen, we still get a reasonable non-zero probability!

🔄 Backoff & Stupid Backoff

Hierarchical fallback for large-scale models

Katz Backoff

Instead of always mixing N-gram orders (interpolation), backoff only uses lower orders when higher orders fail:

Katz Backoff Concept
\[ P_{\text{BO}}(w_n | w_{n-N+1} \ldots w_{n-1}) = \begin{cases} P^*(w_n | w_{n-N+1} \ldots w_{n-1}) & \text{if } C(\ldots) > 0 \\ \alpha \cdot P_{\text{BO}}(w_n | w_{n-N+2} \ldots w_{n-1}) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

\(P^*\) = discounted probability (some mass reserved for unseen)
\(\alpha\) = backoff weight (ensures probabilities sum to 1)

Stupid Backoff

A simpler, non-probabilistic variant that works well at web scale:

Stupid Backoff
\[ S(w_i | w_{i-N+1} \ldots w_{i-1}) = \begin{cases} \frac{C(w_{i-N+1} \ldots w_i)}{C(w_{i-N+1} \ldots w_{i-1})} & \text{if count} > 0 \\ 0.4 \times S(w_i | w_{i-N+2} \ldots w_{i-1}) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Recursively back off with penalty 0.4 (empirically chosen).
Scores don't sum to 1, but relative ranking is preserved.

Numerical Example

📊 Stupid Backoff Example

Computing \( S(\text{eggs} | \text{green ham}) \) with trigram, bigram, unigram:

  • Trigram: C(green ham eggs) = 0 → back off
  • Bigram: S(eggs | ham) = 0.4 × C(ham eggs)/C(ham) = 0.4 × (1/1) = 0.4
  • Result: S = 0.4

If bigram also failed, we'd go to unigram: 0.4 × 0.4 × P(eggs) = 0.16 × P(eggs)

🌐 Web-Scale N-grams

Google released the Web 1T 5-gram corpus (Brants et al., 2007) containing counts from 1 trillion words of web text:

  • 1-grams: 13 million entries
  • 2-grams: 314 million entries
  • 3-grams: 977 million entries
  • 4-grams: 1.3 billion entries
  • 5-grams: 1.2 billion entries

Stupid Backoff was designed for this scale—simple and efficient without normalizing probabilities.

🎯 Perplexity

Evaluating language model quality

What is Perplexity?

Perplexity (PP) measures how "surprised" a model is by test data. Lower perplexity = better model.

🔀 The Branching Factor Analogy

Imagine you're in a maze. At each intersection, you have N doors to choose from. If the maze is confusing (high perplexity), you might face 100 doors. If it's simple (low perplexity), maybe only 2 doors.

Perplexity = average branching factor the model faces when predicting the next word.

Perplexity Definition
\[ \text{PP}(W) = P(w_1 w_2 \ldots w_N)^{-1/N} \]

The inverse probability, normalized by sequence length N.

Perplexity via Cross-Entropy
\[ \text{PP}(W) = 2^{H(W)} \]

Where \( H(W) = -\frac{1}{N} \log_2 P(w_1 \ldots w_N) \) is the cross-entropy.

Calculating Perplexity

📊 Example: Perplexity of "I am Sam"

Earlier we computed \( P(\text{I am Sam}) = 0.111 \) using a bigram model.

The sentence has N=3 words (excluding boundaries).

\[ \text{PP} = (0.111)^{-1/3} = \left(\frac{1}{0.111}\right)^{1/3} = (9.009)^{0.333} \approx \mathbf{2.08} \]

Interpretation: On average, the model has about 2 equally likely choices at each word.

Perplexity in Practice

🎯 Typical Perplexity Values

  • Perfect model: PP = 1 (knows exactly what comes next)
  • Good N-gram model: PP = 50-200 (English text)
  • Bad model (random guessing): PP = V (vocabulary size, e.g., 50,000)
  • Neural LMs (2015): PP = 50-80
  • Modern transformers: PP < 20

✏️ Try It Yourself!

A model assigns probability 0.064 to a 5-word sentence. What is its perplexity?

Solution:

\( \text{PP} = (0.064)^{-1/5} = (1/0.064)^{0.2} = (15.625)^{0.2} \)

\( = \mathbf{2.0} \)

🐍 Python Implementation

Complete, runnable code for N-gram language models

Complete N-gram Language Model

Copy this code into a Python file and run it directly!

"""
N-gram Language Model Implementation
=====================================
Educational implementation with smoothing, perplexity, and generation.
"""

import re
import math
from collections import defaultdict, Counter
from typing import List, Tuple, Dict
import random

class NgramLM:
    """N-gram Language Model with multiple smoothing options."""

    def __init__(self, n=2, smoothing='none', k=1.0, lambdas=None):
        """
        Args:
            n: N-gram order (2=bigram, 3=trigram)
            smoothing: 'none', 'laplace', 'addk', 'interpolation'
            k: smoothing parameter for add-k
            lambdas: weights for interpolation [λ_n, λ_n-1, ..., λ_1]
        """
        self.n = n
        self.smoothing = smoothing
        self.k = k
        self.lambdas = lambdas or [0.6, 0.3, 0.1]
        self.ngram_counts = defaultdict(int)
        self.context_counts = defaultdict(int)
        self.vocab = set()

    def tokenize(self, text: str) -> List[str]:
        """Tokenize text into sentences and words."""
        # Split into sentences
        sentences = re.split(r'[.!?]', text.lower())
        tokens = []
        for sent in sentences:
            words = re.findall(r'\w+', sent)
            if words:
                # Add sentence boundaries
                tokens.extend(['<s>'] * (self.n - 1) + words + ['</s>'])
        return tokens

    def train(self, corpus: str) -> None:
        """Train the N-gram model on a corpus."""
        tokens = self.tokenize(corpus)
        self.vocab = set(tokens)

        # Count N-grams and contexts
        for i in range(len(tokens) - self.n + 1):
            ngram = tuple(tokens[i:i + self.n])
            context = ngram[:-1]
            word = ngram[-1]

            self.ngram_counts[ngram] += 1
            self.context_counts[context] += 1

        # Also count lower-order N-grams for interpolation
        if self.smoothing == 'interpolation':
            for order in range(1, self.n):
                for i in range(len(tokens) - order + 1):
                    ngram = tuple(tokens[i:i + order])
                    if len(ngram) > 1:
                        context = ngram[:-1]
                        self.context_counts[context] = self.context_counts.get(context, 0) + 1

    def prob(self, word: str, context: tuple) -> float:
        """Calculate P(word | context) with smoothing."""
        V = len(self.vocab)
        ngram = context + (word,)

        if self.smoothing == 'none':
            if self.context_counts[context] == 0:
                return 0.0
            return self.ngram_counts[ngram] / self.context_counts[context]

        elif self.smoothing == 'laplace':
            return (self.ngram_counts[ngram] + 1) / (self.context_counts[context] + V)

        elif self.smoothing == 'addk':
            return (self.ngram_counts[ngram] + self.k) / (self.context_counts[context] + self.k * V)

        elif self.smoothing == 'interpolation':
            # Linear interpolation
            prob = 0.0
            for i, lambda_i in enumerate(self.lambdas):
                order = self.n - i
                if order == 1:
                    # Unigram
                    total = sum(self.context_counts.values())
                    prob += lambda_i * (self.ngram_counts[(word,)] / total if total > 0 else 0)
                else:
                    sub_context = context[-(order-1):] if len(context) >= order-1 else context
                    sub_ngram = sub_context + (word,)
                    if self.context_counts[sub_context] > 0:
                        prob += lambda_i * (self.ngram_counts[sub_ngram] / self.context_counts[sub_context])
            return prob

        return 0.0

    def perplexity(self, sentence: str) -> float:
        """Calculate perplexity of a sentence."""
        tokens = self.tokenize(sentence)
        log_prob = 0.0
        count = 0

        for i in range(self.n - 1, len(tokens)):
            context = tuple(tokens[i - self.n + 1:i])
            word = tokens[i]
            p = self.prob(word, context)
            if p > 0:
                log_prob += math.log2(p)
            else:
                log_prob += -20  # Penalty for zero prob
            count += 1

        return 2 ** (-log_prob / count) if count > 0 else float('inf')

    def generate(self, context: tuple, max_words: int = 10) -> str:
        """Generate text given a starting context."""
        result = list(context)

        for _ in range(max_words):
            candidates = [(w, self.prob(w, tuple(result[-(self.n-1):])))
                         for w in self.vocab if w != '<s>']
            candidates = [(w, p) for w, p in candidates if p > 0]

            if not candidates:
                break

            # Sample proportional to probability
            words, probs = zip(*candidates)
            total = sum(probs)
            probs = [p/total for p in probs]
            word = random.choices(words, probs)[0]

            if word == '</s>':
                break
            result.append(word)

        return ' '.join(result)


class StupidBackoff:
    """Stupid Backoff for large-scale N-gram models."""

    def __init__(self, max_n=3, alpha=0.4):
        self.max_n = max_n
        self.alpha = alpha
        self.ngram_counts = defaultdict(int)
        self.context_counts = defaultdict(int)

    def train(self, corpus: str) -> None:
        tokens = re.findall(r'\w+', corpus.lower())

        # Count all N-grams from 1 to max_n
        for n in range(1, self.max_n + 1):
            for i in range(len(tokens) - n + 1):
                ngram = tuple(tokens[i:i + n])
                self.ngram_counts[ngram] += 1
                if n > 1:
                    context = ngram[:-1]
                    self.context_counts[context] += 1

    def score(self, word: str, context: tuple) -> float:
        """Calculate Stupid Backoff score (not a probability)."""
        if not context:
            # Unigram
            return self.ngram_counts[(word,)] / sum(self.ngram_counts.values())

        ngram = context + (word,)
        if self.ngram_counts[ngram] > 0:
            return self.ngram_counts[ngram] / self.context_counts[context]
        else:
            # Back off
            return self.alpha * self.score(word, context[1:])


# Example usage
if __name__ == "__main__":
    corpus = "I am Sam. Sam I am. I do not like green eggs and ham."

    print("=== Bigram Model with Add-k Smoothing ===")
    model = NgramLM(n=2, smoothing='addk', k=0.1)
    model.train(corpus)

    test_sentence = "I am Sam"
    pp = model.perplexity(test_sentence)
    print(f"Perplexity of '{test_sentence}': {pp:.2f}")

    print("\n=== Next Word Predictions after 'I am' ===")
    context = ('i', 'am')
    predictions = [(w, model.prob(w, context)) for w in model.vocab if w != '<s>']
    predictions.sort(key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
    for w, p in predictions[:5]:
        print(f"  {w}: {p:.4f}")

    print("\n=== Text Generation ===")
    generated = model.generate(('<s>', 'i'), max_words=10)
    print(f"Generated: {generated}")

    print("\n=== Stupid Backoff ===")
    backoff = StupidBackoff(max_n=3, alpha=0.4)
    backoff.train(corpus)
    score = backoff.score('sam', ('i', 'am'))
    print(f"Score('sam' | 'i am'): {score:.4f}")

📚 References

Key works in statistical language modeling

Primary Sources

  • Jurafsky, D., & Martin, J. H. (2024). Speech and Language Processing (3rd ed.), Chapter 3: N-gram Language Models. Draft available at: https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/

    The definitive textbook on N-gram models, covering MLE, smoothing, backoff, and evaluation in depth.
  • Shannon, C. E. (1951). "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English." Bell System Technical Journal, 30(1), 50-64.

    Shannon's groundbreaking work applying information theory to language, introducing perplexity and demonstrating that English has about 1-1.5 bits per character of entropy.
  • Chen, S. F., & Goodman, J. (1999). "An Empirical Study of Smoothing Techniques for Language Modeling." Computer Speech & Language, 13(4), 359-394.

    Comprehensive empirical comparison of smoothing methods (Laplace, Katz backoff, interpolation, Kneser-Ney). Shows that Kneser-Ney and modified Kneser-Ney perform best.
  • Brants, T., Popat, A. C., Xu, P., Och, F. J., & Dean, J. (2007). "Large Language Models in Machine Translation." Proceedings of EMNLP-CoNLL, 858-867.

    Introduced Stupid Backoff and demonstrated that scaling N-gram models to trillions of tokens improves translation quality. Released the Google Web 1T 5-gram corpus.
