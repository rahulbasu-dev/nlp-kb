Understanding GloVe

Global Vectors for Word Representation combines the best of count-based and prediction-based methods to learn word embeddings that capture semantic meaning from global co-occurrence statistics [1].

Bridging Count-Based and Neural Methods

💡 Intuition

Understanding the core idea behind GloVe through analogies and real-world examples

What is GloVe?

GloVe stands for Global Vectors for Word Representation. Developed at Stanford in 2014, it's a word embedding technique that learns vector representations by analyzing how often words co-occur across an entire text corpus [1].

The key insight is powerful: The ratio of co-occurrence probabilities between words encodes semantic relationships more effectively than raw counts or probabilities alone [1].

🏢 The Social Network Analogy

Imagine understanding people by who they spend time with:

  • Direct counting tells you "Alice spent time with Bob 50 times" - but is that a lot?
  • Ratios reveal meaning: "Alice spends 10x more time with Bob than with Carol, but Charlie spends equal time with both." Now you understand relationships!

GloVe does exactly this for words - it learns meaning from the relative patterns of who "hangs out" with whom.

The Co-occurrence Matrix

"Who appears near whom?"

GloVe starts by building a giant matrix that counts how often each word appears near every other word in the corpus.

📝 Example

In "The cat sat on the mat", if we use a window of 1:

  • "cat" co-occurs with "the" and "sat"
  • "sat" co-occurs with "cat" and "on"

Matrix entry X[i,j]:

X[i,j] = count of word j appearing near word i

The Power of Ratios

"Relative relationships reveal meaning"

Instead of looking at raw counts, GloVe examines the ratio of probabilities. This ratio reveals semantic relationships that raw numbers hide.

📝 Classic Example: ice vs. steam

Consider P(k|ice) / P(k|steam) for different words k:

  • k = "solid": ratio is LARGE (ice-related)
  • k = "gas": ratio is SMALL (steam-related)
  • k = "water": ratio is ~1 (both related)

The ratio encodes: "Is k more associated with ice, steam, or both equally?"

Best of Both Worlds

🔭 GloVe's Unique Position

GloVe bridges two major approaches to word embeddings [1][5]:

  • Count-based methods (LSA, HAL): Use global corpus statistics, efficient, but miss nuanced relationships [5]
  • Prediction-based methods (Word2Vec): Learn local context patterns, capture analogy relationships, but don't use global statistics directly [4]

GloVe combines both: It uses global co-occurrence statistics but learns vectors through a prediction-style objective! [1]

The Core Idea [1]

wi · wj + biases = log(Xij)

GloVe learns word vectors such that their dot product approximates the log of how often they co-occur [1]:

  • High co-occurrence = high dot product = similar vectors
  • Low co-occurrence = low dot product = different vectors
  • The log transform prevents very frequent pairs from dominating

📊 The Power of Ratios

The ratio P(k|ice)/P(k|steam) perfectly captures semantic relationships:

  • Ratio >> 1: k is more associated with "ice" (like "solid", "cold")
  • Ratio << 1: k is more associated with "steam" (like "gas", "hot")
  • Ratio ~ 1: k is equally related to both (like "water") or unrelated to both

This is exactly what GloVe's vectors are trained to capture!

Step 3: What GloVe Learns

GloVe trains word vectors so that:

Vector Relationship Goal

(wice - wsteam) · wk ≈ log(P(k|ice) / P(k|steam))

The difference vector between "ice" and "steam" should have:

  • Positive dot product with "solid" (ice-related)
  • Negative dot product with "gas" (steam-related)
  • Near zero dot product with "water" (both-related)

🎯 The King-Queen Analogy

This is why GloVe (like Word2Vec) captures analogies [1][4]:

king - man + woman ≈ queen

The co-occurrence ratios encode gender relationships consistently across the corpus, and GloVe's vectors preserve these ratios through their dot products! [1]

🔢 Mathematical Foundations

The formal definitions and formulas behind GloVe

Co-occurrence Probability

Let \( X \) be the word-word co-occurrence matrix, where \( X_{ij} \) is the number of times word \( j \) appears in the context of word \( i \).

Co-occurrence Probability
\[ P_{ij} = P(j|i) = \frac{X_{ij}}{X_i} \]
Where \( X_i = \sum_k X_{ik} \) is the total number of times any word appears in the context of word \( i \).

The Ratio that Captures Meaning

The key insight of GloVe is that ratios of co-occurrence probabilities encode semantic relationships [1]:

Probability Ratio
\[ \frac{P_{ik}}{P_{jk}} = \frac{P(k|i)}{P(k|j)} \]

For words \( i = \text{ice} \) and \( j = \text{steam} \):

  • If \( k = \text{solid} \): ratio is large (ice-related)
  • If \( k = \text{gas} \): ratio is small (steam-related)
  • If \( k = \text{water} \): ratio ≈ 1 (related to both)

The GloVe Model

GloVe learns word vectors \( w_i \) and context vectors \( \tilde{w}_j \) such that their dot product relates to the log co-occurrence [1]:

GloVe Relationship
\[ w_i^T \tilde{w}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j = \log(X_{ij}) \]

Where:

  • \( w_i \) = word vector for word \( i \)
  • \( \tilde{w}_j \) = context vector for word \( j \)
  • \( b_i, \tilde{b}_j \) = bias terms

❓ Why Logarithm? [1]

  1. Converts multiplicative to additive: Ratios become differences in log space
  2. Enables vector arithmetic: \( w_i - w_j \) captures the ratio relationship
  3. Handles scale: Prevents very frequent co-occurrences from dominating

The GloVe Objective Function

GloVe Cost Function [1]
\[ J = \sum_{i,j=1}^{V} f(X_{ij}) \left( w_i^T \tilde{w}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j - \log X_{ij} \right)^2 \]

This is a weighted least squares objective where [1]:

  • We minimize the squared error between the dot product and log co-occurrence
  • \( f(X_{ij}) \) is a weighting function that controls the importance of each co-occurrence
  • \( V \) is the vocabulary size

The Weighting Function f(x)

The weighting function prevents frequent but uninformative word pairs from dominating [1]:

Weighting Function [1]
\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} (x/x_{\max})^\alpha & \text{if } x < x_{\max} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Typical values: \( x_{\max} = 100 \), \( \alpha = 0.75 \) [1]

  • Rare co-occurrences: Get lower weight (might be noise)
  • Frequent co-occurrences: Capped at weight 1 (don't over-emphasize)
  • \( \alpha = 0.75 \): Sublinear scaling prevents very frequent pairs from dominating

Understanding the Components

Component Symbol Dimension Purpose
Word vectors\( W \)\( V \times d \)Main embeddings for each word
Context vectors\( \tilde{W} \)\( V \times d \)Separate embeddings for context role
Word biases\( b \)\( V \times 1 \)Capture word frequency effects
Context biases\( \tilde{b} \)\( V \times 1 \)Capture context frequency effects
Co-occurrence matrix\( X \)\( V \times V \)Input statistics from corpus

⚠️ Zero Co-occurrences

When \( X_{ij} = 0 \), the log is undefined. GloVe handles this by:

  • Only summing over non-zero entries (sparse matrix)
  • The weighting function \( f(0) = 0 \) means zero entries don't contribute

🔄 GloVe Training Pipeline

Visual representation of the complete GloVe training process

Pipeline Steps Explained

1

Preprocessing

Tokenize text into words, lowercase, optionally remove rare words. Build vocabulary of unique words.

2

Build Co-occurrence Matrix

Scan through corpus with a context window (typically 10-15 words). For each word pair within the window, increment X[i,j]. Often weighted by distance (closer = higher count).

3

Initialize Parameters

Randomly initialize word vectors W, context vectors W_context, and bias terms b, b_context. Typical dimension d = 50-300.

4

Training Loop

Forward: Compute dot product + biases. Loss: Weighted squared error vs log(X_ij). Backward: Compute gradients. Update: Use AdaGrad optimizer.

5

Final Embeddings

The final word vectors can be W alone, or W + W_context (often performs slightly better). These are your GloVe embeddings!

"""
GloVe Implementation from Scratch
=================================
Educational implementation - clear, commented, runnable.
Learns word embeddings from co-occurrence statistics.
"""

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter, defaultdict
import re

class GloVe:
    """GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation."""

    def __init__(self, embedding_dim=50, x_max=100, alpha=0.75,
                 learning_rate=0.05, epochs=100, window_size=5):
        """
        Args:
            embedding_dim: Dimension of word vectors
            x_max: Maximum co-occurrence count for weighting
            alpha: Exponent for weighting function
            learning_rate: Learning rate for AdaGrad
            epochs: Number of training iterations
            window_size: Context window size (each side)
        """
        self.embedding_dim = embedding_dim
        self.x_max = x_max
        self.alpha = alpha
        self.lr = learning_rate
        self.epochs = epochs
        self.window_size = window_size

        # Will be set during training
        self.vocab = {}
        self.inverse_vocab = {}
        self.W = None  # Word vectors
        self.W_ctx = None  # Context vectors
        self.b = None  # Word biases
        self.b_ctx = None  # Context biases

    def tokenize(self, text):
        """Simple tokenization: lowercase and extract words."""
        return re.findall(r'[a-z]+', text.lower())

    def build_vocab(self, corpus, min_count=1):
        """Build vocabulary from corpus."""
        word_counts = Counter()
        for sentence in corpus:
            tokens = self.tokenize(sentence)
            word_counts.update(tokens)

        # Filter by minimum count
        self.vocab = {word: i for i, (word, count) in
                      enumerate(word_counts.items()) if count >= min_count}
        self.inverse_vocab = {i: word for word, i in self.vocab.items()}
        return len(self.vocab)

    def build_cooccurrence_matrix(self, corpus):
        """Build word co-occurrence matrix from corpus."""
        V = len(self.vocab)
        cooccurrence = defaultdict(lambda: defaultdict(float))

        for sentence in corpus:
            tokens = self.tokenize(sentence)
            # Filter to vocabulary
            token_ids = [self.vocab[t] for t in tokens if t in self.vocab]

            for i, center_id in enumerate(token_ids):
                # Look at context window
                start = max(0, i - self.window_size)
                end = min(len(token_ids), i + self.window_size + 1)

                for j in range(start, end):
                    if i != j:
                        context_id = token_ids[j]
                        distance = abs(i - j)
                        # Weight by inverse distance
                        cooccurrence[center_id][context_id] += 1.0 / distance

        # Convert to list of (i, j, count) tuples
        cooccurrences = []
        for i in cooccurrence:
            for j in cooccurrence[i]:
                cooccurrences.append((i, j, cooccurrence[i][j]))

        return cooccurrences

    def weighting_function(self, x):
        """Weighting function f(x) to reduce impact of very frequent pairs."""
        if x < self.x_max:
            return (x / self.x_max) ** self.alpha
        return 1.0

    def train(self, corpus, verbose=True):
        """Train GloVe embeddings on corpus."""
        # Build vocabulary
        V = self.build_vocab(corpus)
        if verbose:
            print(f"Vocabulary size: {V}")

        # Build co-occurrence matrix
        cooccurrences = self.build_cooccurrence_matrix(corpus)
        if verbose:
            print(f"Non-zero co-occurrences: {len(cooccurrences)}")

        # Initialize parameters
        self.W = (np.random.rand(V, self.embedding_dim) - 0.5) / self.embedding_dim
        self.W_ctx = (np.random.rand(V, self.embedding_dim) - 0.5) / self.embedding_dim
        self.b = np.zeros(V)
        self.b_ctx = np.zeros(V)

        # AdaGrad accumulators
        grad_sq_W = np.ones_like(self.W)
        grad_sq_W_ctx = np.ones_like(self.W_ctx)
        grad_sq_b = np.ones_like(self.b)
        grad_sq_b_ctx = np.ones_like(self.b_ctx)

        # Training loop
        for epoch in range(self.epochs):
            total_loss = 0.0
            np.random.shuffle(cooccurrences)

            for i, j, x_ij in cooccurrences:
                if x_ij == 0:
                    continue

                # Forward pass
                dot_product = np.dot(self.W[i], self.W_ctx[j])
                prediction = dot_product + self.b[i] + self.b_ctx[j]
                target = np.log(x_ij)
                diff = prediction - target

                # Weighted loss
                weight = self.weighting_function(x_ij)
                loss = weight * diff * diff
                total_loss += loss

                # Gradients
                grad_common = weight * diff

                # Update word vector
                grad_W = grad_common * self.W_ctx[j]
                grad_sq_W[i] += grad_W ** 2
                self.W[i] -= self.lr * grad_W / np.sqrt(grad_sq_W[i])

                # Update context vector
                grad_W_ctx = grad_common * self.W[i]
                grad_sq_W_ctx[j] += grad_W_ctx ** 2
                self.W_ctx[j] -= self.lr * grad_W_ctx / np.sqrt(grad_sq_W_ctx[j])

                # Update biases
                grad_sq_b[i] += grad_common ** 2
                self.b[i] -= self.lr * grad_common / np.sqrt(grad_sq_b[i])

                grad_sq_b_ctx[j] += grad_common ** 2
                self.b_ctx[j] -= self.lr * grad_common / np.sqrt(grad_sq_b_ctx[j])

            if verbose and (epoch + 1) % 10 == 0:
                print(f"Epoch {epoch+1}/{self.epochs}, Loss: {total_loss:.4f}")

        return self

    def get_embedding(self, word):
        """Get embedding for a word (W + W_ctx combined)."""
        if word not in self.vocab:
            return None
        idx = self.vocab[word]
        return self.W[idx] + self.W_ctx[idx]

    def most_similar(self, word, top_n=5):
        """Find most similar words by cosine similarity."""
        if word not in self.vocab:
            return []

        vec = self.get_embedding(word)
        vec = vec / np.linalg.norm(vec)

        similarities = []
        for w, idx in self.vocab.items():
            if w != word:
                other_vec = self.get_embedding(w)
                other_vec = other_vec / np.linalg.norm(other_vec)
                sim = np.dot(vec, other_vec)
                similarities.append((w, sim))

        similarities.sort(key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
        return similarities[:top_n]


# Example usage
if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Sample corpus (in practice, use much larger data)
    corpus = [
        "the king rules the kingdom",
        "the queen rules with the king",
        "the prince is the son of the king",
        "the princess is the daughter of the queen",
        "a man works in the field",
        "a woman works in the castle",
        "the king is a man",
        "the queen is a woman",
        "the prince will become king",
        "the princess will become queen",
    ] * 100  # Repeat for more training data

    # Train GloVe
    glove = GloVe(embedding_dim=30, epochs=50, window_size=3)
    glove.train(corpus)

    # Test embeddings
    print("\nMost similar to 'king':")
    for word, sim in glove.most_similar('king'):
        print(f"  {word}: {sim:.4f}")

    print("\nMost similar to 'queen':")
    for word, sim in glove.most_similar('queen'):
        print(f"  {word}: {sim:.4f}")

🔧 GloVe vs Word2Vec

Understanding when to use each method

Key Differences

Aspect GloVe [1] Word2Vec (Skip-gram) [4]
Training Approach Factorizes co-occurrence matrix [1][2] Predicts context words
Statistics Used Global corpus statistics [1] Local context windows
Training Data Pre-computed co-occurrence counts Raw text (online learning)
Memory Stores co-occurrence matrix Streams through corpus
Parallelization Easy (matrix factorization) Requires special handling
Analogy Tasks Excellent Excellent
Rare Words Explicit weighting function Negative sampling handles this

When to Use Each

Choose GloVe When...

  • You have a fixed, known corpus
  • Memory for co-occurrence matrix is available
  • You want interpretable training (log-bilinear)
  • You prefer batch training over online
  • Corpus statistics are stable

Choose Word2Vec When...

  • Corpus is very large or streaming
  • Memory is limited
  • You need online/incremental updates
  • Subword information matters (fastText variant)
  • Simpler implementation preferred

💡 In Practice

Both methods produce high-quality embeddings that perform similarly on most tasks [3]. The choice often comes down to:

  • Pre-trained availability: Both have excellent pre-trained models (GloVe from Stanford, Word2Vec from Google)
  • Implementation preference: Word2Vec is often easier to train on custom data
  • Theoretical interest: GloVe's explicit matrix factorization is more interpretable [2]

The GloVe Paper's Key Insight

🎯 Why Ratios Matter

The GloVe authors showed that Word2Vec's skip-gram implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI matrix [2]:

w_i · w_j ≈ PMI(i,j) - log(k)

GloVe makes this explicit and shows that working with probability ratios directly leads to better semantic analogies [1]. The ratio P(k|ice)/P(k|steam) directly encodes meaning in a way that raw probabilities don't!

A Tiny 4-Word Corpus

Let's work through GloVe with the smallest possible meaningful example [1]. Consider a corpus with just 4 unique words:

📚 Our Minimal Corpus (window size = 1)

  • Sentence 1: "ice cold"
  • Sentence 2: "steam hot"
  • Sentence 3: "ice water"
  • Sentence 4: "steam water"

Vocabulary: {ice, cold, steam, hot, water} - 5 words total

Step 1: Build the Co-occurrence Matrix X

With window size = 1, we count adjacent word pairs:

X ice cold steam hot water
ice01001
cold10000
steam00011
hot00100
water10100

Row sums give us Xi (total context count for word i):

  • Xice = 0 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 1 = 2
  • Xcold = 1 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 1
  • Xsteam = 0 + 0 + 0 + 1 + 1 = 2
  • Xhot = 0 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 1
  • Xwater = 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 2

Step 2: Calculate Co-occurrence Probabilities P(k|word)

The probability P(k|i) = Xik / Xi represents how likely word k appears in the context of word i [1]:

P(k|word) k = cold k = hot k = water
P(k|ice) 1/2 = 0.50 0/2 = 0.00 1/2 = 0.50
P(k|steam) 0/2 = 0.00 1/2 = 0.50 1/2 = 0.50

The ratio P(k|ice)/P(k|steam) reveals semantic relationships [1]:

Probe Word k P(k|ice) P(k|steam) Ratio Interpretation
cold 0.50 0.00 (use 0.001) 500 (large) Strongly associated with "ice"
hot 0.00 (use 0.001) 0.50 0.002 (small) Strongly associated with "steam"
water 0.50 0.50 1.0 Equally associated with both

💡 The Key Insight [1]

The ratio perfectly captures semantic relationships:

  • Ratio >> 1: Probe word is more ice-related (like "cold")
  • Ratio << 1: Probe word is more steam-related (like "hot")
  • Ratio ~ 1: Probe word is equally related to both (like "water")

GloVe learns vectors where (wice - wsteam) · wk approximates log(ratio)!

One Iteration of GloVe Training

Let's trace through one complete training step for the word pair (ice, cold) [1]:

Initial Setup (2D vectors for simplicity)

📋 Initial Random Vectors and Biases

Word vectors (W):
  w_ice   = [0.5, -0.3]
  w_cold  = [0.2, 0.4]

Context vectors (W_ctx):
  w_ctx_ice  = [-0.1, 0.6]
  w_ctx_cold = [0.3, -0.2]

Biases:
  b_ice = 0.1,  b_cold = -0.05
  b_ctx_ice = 0.05,  b_ctx_cold = 0.02

Co-occurrence:
  X[ice, cold] = 1

Step 1: Forward Pass - Compute Prediction

For the pair (i=ice, j=cold), compute the dot product plus biases:

Prediction Calculation

Dot product: wice · wctx_cold

= [0.5, -0.3] · [0.3, -0.2]

= (0.5 × 0.3) + (-0.3 × -0.2)

= 0.15 + 0.06 = 0.21


Full prediction:

pred = dot + bice + bctx_cold

= 0.21 + 0.1 + 0.02 = 0.33

Step 2: Compute Target and Error

The target is log(Xij), and we compute the error:

Error Calculation

Target: log(X[ice, cold]) = log(1) = 0.0


Error (diff):

diff = prediction - target

= 0.33 - 0.0 = 0.33

Step 3: Calculate Weighted Loss

Apply the weighting function f(x) to control the contribution of this pair [1]:

Loss Calculation

Weighting function (with xmax=100, α=0.75):

f(X[ice,cold]) = f(1) = (1/100)0.75

= 0.010.75 = 0.0316


Weighted loss for this pair:

L = f(x) × diff2

= 0.0316 × (0.33)2

= 0.0316 × 0.1089 = 0.00344

Step 4: Compute Gradients

The gradient of the loss with respect to each parameter:

Gradient Calculation

Common gradient factor:

grad_common = 2 × f(x) × diff

= 2 × 0.0316 × 0.33 = 0.0209


Gradient for wice:

∇wice = grad_common × wctx_cold

= 0.0209 × [0.3, -0.2]

= [0.00627, -0.00418]


Gradient for wctx_cold:

∇wctx_cold = grad_common × wice

= 0.0209 × [0.5, -0.3]

= [0.01045, -0.00627]


Gradient for biases:

∇bice = grad_common = 0.0209

∇bctx_cold = grad_common = 0.0209

Step 5: Update Parameters (using simple SGD with lr=0.05)

Parameter Updates

Update wice:

wice = wice - lr × ∇wice

= [0.5, -0.3] - 0.05 × [0.00627, -0.00418]

= [0.5 - 0.000314, -0.3 + 0.000209]

= [0.49969, -0.29979]


Update wctx_cold:

wctx_cold = [0.3, -0.2] - 0.05 × [0.01045, -0.00627]

= [0.29948, -0.19969]


Update biases:

bice = 0.1 - 0.05 × 0.0209 = 0.0990

bctx_cold = 0.02 - 0.05 × 0.0209 = 0.0190

💡 What Just Happened?

Since our prediction (0.33) was higher than the target (0.0), the gradient update:

  • Slightly reduced the dot product between wice and wctx_cold
  • Slightly reduced both biases

Over many iterations across all word pairs, the vectors converge to represent semantic relationships!

📚 References & Bibliography

Key papers and resources for further study

Primary References

[1] The Original GloVe Paper

Pennington, J., Socher, R., & Manning, C. D. (2014). "GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation." Proceedings of the 2014 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing (EMNLP), pages 1532-1543.

The foundational paper introducing GloVe, demonstrating how ratio of co-occurrence probabilities can capture semantic meaning, and presenting the weighted least-squares objective function.

PDF | Project Page

[2] Word2Vec as Implicit Matrix Factorization

Levy, O., & Goldberg, Y. (2014). "Neural Word Embedding as Implicit Matrix Factorization." Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NIPS), 27.

Shows that Skip-gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI matrix, providing theoretical connections between neural and count-based methods.

[3] Improving Distributional Similarity

Levy, O., Goldberg, Y., & Dagan, I. (2015). "Improving Distributional Similarity with Lessons Learned from Word Embeddings." Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics (TACL), 3, 211-225.

Systematic comparison showing that much of word embeddings' success comes from hyperparameter tuning, with practical recommendations for both neural and count-based methods.

[4] Count vs. Predict Comparison

Baroni, M., Dinu, G., & Kruszewski, G. (2014). "Don't count, predict! A systematic comparison of context-counting vs. context-predicting semantic vectors." Proceedings of the 52nd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL), pages 238-247.

Comprehensive evaluation showing prediction-based models (like Word2Vec) outperforming count-based models across various semantic tasks, motivating hybrid approaches like GloVe.

[5] Vector Space Models Survey

Turney, P. D., & Pantel, P. (2010). "From Frequency to Meaning: Vector Space Models of Semantics." Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research (JAIR), 37, 141-188.

Comprehensive survey of vector space models for semantics, covering the theoretical foundations that underpin both count-based methods and modern word embeddings.
